**Short answer:** three things shape the Indian move more than anything else. You need an MVV entry visa that Americans and Britons do not, your Indian driving licence cannot be exchanged unless the 30% ruling applies to you, and your Indian financial life — savings, accounts, property income — has to be restructured for non-resident status rather than simply left behind.

India is now the largest source country for non-EU knowledge migrants coming to the Netherlands, and the route is well-worn: an IND-recognised sponsor files your permit, you collect an MVV, you land, you register. Where Indian movers lose money and time is in the four areas below, none of which the employer's relocation package usually covers.

For the "should I move" question — salaries, cost of living, work culture — read [Netherlands vs India for expats](/guides/integration/netherlands-vs-india-expats-2026/). This guide is the execution.

## Your Timeline: Three Months Out to Month One

**Three months before.** The employer confirms it is an IND-recognised sponsor and starts the combined MVV-plus-permit application. Simultaneously, request the documents that take longest from India: birth certificate and marriage certificate with an apostille, and a police clearance certificate if your employer or sector asks for one. India is a party to the Apostille Convention, so an apostille from the competent Indian authority normally replaces embassy legalisation — verify the requirement for your specific document with the Dutch mission, since the rules differ by document type. Our [apostille and legalisation guide](/guides/legal/apostille-legalization-netherlands-2026/) explains what the gemeente will accept.

**Two months before.** Permit approved, book the MVV appointment at the Dutch mission. Start the housing search in parallel: the Dutch rental market, not the visa, is what delays most arrivals. Read [finding housing](/guides/housing/finding-housing-netherlands-expats-2026/) and [rental scams](/guides/housing/rental-scams-netherlands-expats-2026/) — advance-fee scams disproportionately target overseas applicants who cannot view a property.

**One month before.** Collect the MVV. Convert your Indian accounts to NRE/NRO status with your bank, or at least get written confirmation of the process. Plan the first large remittance under the LRS route. Book a gemeente appointment as soon as you have an address — Amsterdam runs four to six weeks, smaller cities one to two.

**Arrival week.** Gemeente registration and [BSN](/guides/legal/bsn-registration-guide-2026/), residence card collection, TB test appointment at the GGD if it applies to you, [DigiD](/guides/legal/digid-guide-expats-2026/).

**First month.** Dutch bank account, basisverzekering, and the 30% ruling application. Follow the [first 30 days checklist](/guides/legal/first-30-days-netherlands-expat-checklist-2026/).

## Difference 1: The MVV Step Nobody Else in the Tech Team Has

The IND's MVV-exemption list is short and India is not on it. So while a colleague relocating from Austin or Manchester simply books a flight once the permit is approved, you have an extra consular stage: the MVV sticker, collected in person at the Dutch mission in India, valid for 90 days for entry.

Plan for it rather than around it. The application is filed by your employer as a combined application, so you are not chasing two separate processes, but the appointment slot at the mission is a real scheduling constraint and it is the most common reason an Indian start date slips by a month.

**Salary criteria.** For the highly skilled migrant permit in the period 1 July to 31 December 2026, the IND's gross monthly salary criteria excluding the statutory 8% holiday allowance are €5,942 for applicants aged 30 and over, €4,357 for under-30s, and €3,122 under the reduced criterion for recent graduates and former orientation-year permit holders. Two things to check in your offer letter: that the figure is exclusive of the 8% holiday allowance, and that the amount is the one valid on your start date rather than the one from the previous half-year. See the [highly skilled migrant guide](/guides/legal/highly-skilled-migrant-visa-netherlands-2026/) and use the [salary checker](/tools/salary-checker/).

## Difference 2: The 30% Ruling Is Also Your Driving Licence

For Indian movers the 30% ruling is not only a tax benefit. It is the only realistic route to a Dutch driving licence without sitting a Dutch exam.

The RDW's exchange list of non-EU/EFTA countries reads, as of August 2026: Andorra, Alberta, Québec, Gibraltar, Great Britain, Guernsey, the Isle of Man, Israel, Japan, Jersey, Monaco, Northern Ireland, Singapore, Chinese Taipei, South Korea and the former Netherlands Antilles. India is not on it, and the RDW states that where a country is not on the list, exchange is not possible. The exception, in the RDW's own words: if you are in the Netherlands for work as a highly skilled migrant and the expat scheme applies to you, you may exchange a licence from any country.

**So the sequence matters.** You may drive on your Indian licence for 185 days after relocating. If your 30% ruling is granted — the application goes to the Belastingdienst and must be filed within four months of your first working day to be backdated — use it to exchange the licence well inside that window, not in year four. If the ruling is refused or you are not eligible, you are on the CBR route: theory exam, practical exam and lessons, which realistically means several months and a four-figure budget. Both routes are set out in the [licence exchange guide](/guides/daily-life/exchange-driving-license-netherlands-2026/) and the [CBR guide](/guides/daily-life/dutch-driving-license-cbr-guide-2026/).

**Eligibility, briefly.** The 30% ruling has nothing to do with nationality. It requires that you were recruited from abroad and that you lived more than 150 km from the Dutch border for at least 16 of the 24 months before your Dutch employment started. Anywhere in India clears the distance test with room to spare, so for Indian movers the live conditions are the recruitment requirement and the salary norm. If you were already studying in the Netherlands, count those months carefully — Dutch months count against you. See the [eligibility guide](/guides/finance/30-percent-ruling-eligibility-guide-2026/).

## Difference 3: Diploma Evaluation and Regulated Professions

Dutch employers in the Eindhoven semiconductor cluster and the Amsterdam tech scene read Indian CVs fluently, and for most engineering and IT roles no formal recognition step exists. The friction appears elsewhere:

- **Regulated professions** — medicine, nursing, pharmacy, teaching, law, several engineering-adjacent certifications — require a Dutch recognition procedure before you may practise, regardless of how strong your qualification is.
- **Dutch master's or PhD admission** often requires a formal comparison of your bachelor's degree.
- **Integration and some public-sector roles** require a credential evaluation as a document.

IDW is the body that issues these evaluations: a comparison of your foreign diploma with the Dutch education system, stating which Dutch level it corresponds to. IDW's own conditions are worth knowing before you apply — the diploma must be nationally recognised, the programme at least 1,000 hours, one diploma per application, and no evaluation of work experience or short courses. IDW currently warns of longer processing times because of application volume, so if your plan depends on the document, start it early rather than after you arrive.

Bring original degree certificates and transcripts with you. Requesting attested copies from an Indian university after you have moved is a genuinely miserable process.

## Difference 4: Restructuring Your Indian Finances

This is where Indian movers most often lose money quietly, because nothing forces the issue and nobody at the employer mentions it.

**Residency status.** Once you move, your Indian status changes and your resident savings and current accounts should be converted to NRE or NRO accounts. The short version: NRE holds foreign-earned money and is freely repatriable; NRO holds Indian-sourced income such as rent or dividends and is not freely repatriable. Getting the classification wrong makes bringing your own money out of India later much harder than it needs to be.

**The remittance route out.** Sending savings from India to the Netherlands runs through the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which caps the amount a resident individual may remit per financial year and applies tax collected at source on outward remittances. Both the cap and the TCS treatment have changed more than once, so ask your bank for the current figures rather than trusting a blog — including this one.

**Indian income after you leave.** Rental income from an Indian flat, interest on Indian deposits and Indian capital gains do not stop existing when you become a Dutch tax resident. The India–Netherlands double taxation avoidance agreement determines which country taxes what, and Dutch box 3 wealth taxation may bring the Indian assets into scope. The [Dutch tax system guide](/guides/finance/dutch-tax-system-expats-guide-2026/) and the [box 3 guide](/guides/finance/dutch-box-3-tax-savings-expats-2026/) explain the Dutch side; the treaty interaction is worth an adviser in year one.

### The INR corridor: sending money home

Almost every Indian expat I work with sends money to India regularly — parental support, an EMI, a property purchase, a sibling's education. This is the one recurring cost of the move, and the difference between doing it well and badly compounds every month.

The cost of an EUR-to-INR transfer sits in two places: the stated fee, and the exchange rate spread over the mid-market rate. Bank remittance channels typically show a modest fee and take the margin in the rate, which is why two providers quoting "low fees" can differ by a meaningful amount on the same transfer. Compare the amount that actually lands in the Indian account, not the fee.

A multi-currency account also solves the arrival problem: you can open one before you leave India, hold EUR, and receive your first salary into a European IBAN while your Dutch bank application is still in progress.

## Healthcare and Insurance

There is no reciprocal health arrangement between India and the Netherlands, so your Indian corporate cover ends at the airport. Dutch basic health insurance is compulsory within four months of your municipal registration, backdated to the registration date if you sign up inside the window; miss it and the CAK warns you, fines you (around €469 in 2026 — check the current amount) and can assign you a policy.

What surprises Indian arrivals most is not the price but the model. You pay a monthly premium and an annual deductible, the huisarts gatekeeps all specialist access, prescribing is conservative by Indian corporate-hospital standards, and adult dental care is outside the basic package. ## Integration and Language

Non-EU residence permits generally bring an integration (inburgering) obligation, with exemptions for certain permit types — highly skilled migrants are typically exempt while on that permit, but the position changes if you switch permits or apply for permanent residence or naturalisation. Check your own status with your gemeente rather than assuming, and read the [inburgering guide](/guides/integration/inburgeringsexamen-preparation-guide-2026/) and the [Dutch nationality guide](/guides/legal/dutch-nationality-guide-expats-2026/).

You can work in English in most knowledge-migrant roles. Dutch becomes the constraint at the gemeente counter, in tenancy documents, in school communication and in social life outside the office. ## The Mistake Indian Movers Make Most Often

Leaving the Indian side untouched. The Dutch side of the move is well documented and your employer usually helps with it. Nobody helps with the NRE/NRO conversion, the LRS remittance planning, the Indian rental income that now needs treaty analysis, or the degree certificates you will need attested copies of three years from now. Do those in the month before you fly, while you are still physically in India and still a resident for your bank's purposes.
