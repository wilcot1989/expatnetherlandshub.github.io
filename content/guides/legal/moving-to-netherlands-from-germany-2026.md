**Short answer:** three things define the German move, and only one of them is bureaucracy. There is no visa and no permit — you register at a municipality and get a BSN. Your driving licence and your professional qualifications largely travel with you. And the 30% ruling, which colleagues arriving from India or the US take for granted, is probably not available to you, because most of western Germany sits inside the 150 kilometre exclusion radius.

German movers arrive expecting the Netherlands to be a slightly flatter, slightly wetter version of home and are usually right about daily life. Where they get caught out is in the three or four places where the two systems are structured differently rather than just labelled differently.

For the cost-of-living and culture comparison, see [Netherlands vs Germany for expats](/guides/integration/netherlands-vs-germany-expats-2026/) and [Netherlands vs Germany cost of living](/guides/integration/netherlands-vs-germany-cost-of-living-2026/). This guide is the move itself.

## Your Timeline: Three Months Out to Month One

**Three months before.** No immigration process to start, which frees the whole timeline for the thing that actually blocks German movers: housing. Dutch rental supply in the Randstad is tighter than in almost any German city, deposits and agency practices differ, and a Dutch landlord will usually want a Dutch employment contract. Read [finding housing](/guides/housing/finding-housing-netherlands-expats-2026/) and [best cities for expats](/guides/housing/best-cities-netherlands-expats-2026/).

**Two months before.** Get your 150 km position measured (see below) before you negotiate salary, because whether the 30% ruling applies changes your net pay materially and it is a fact about your old address, not something you can fix later. Ask your prospective employer whether the offer assumes the ruling.

**One month before.** Book the gemeente appointment as soon as you have an address — Amsterdam runs four to six weeks, smaller cities one to two. Arrange your German Abmeldung for the right date: it ends your German registration, which in turn affects the Rundfunkbeitrag, your German tax residency and your statutory insurance. Do not deregister before you have the Dutch registration slot booked.

**Arrival week.** Gemeente registration, which produces your [BSN](/guides/legal/bsn-registration-guide-2026/) on the spot, then [DigiD](/guides/legal/digid-guide-expats-2026/).

**First month.** Dutch bank account, Dutch health insurance, energy and internet contracts. The [first 30 days checklist](/guides/legal/first-30-days-netherlands-expat-checklist-2026/) has the order.

## Difference 1: Registration Instead of Immigration

There is no IND application, no MVV, no sponsor and no permit. Free movement means the Dutch state's interest in you starts at the municipal counter.

What you do owe is registration in the BRP. The legal requirement is to register within five days of moving in if you are staying longer than four months; in practice enforcement is loose but the appointment queue is not, so the constraint is the gemeente's calendar rather than the deadline. You receive your BSN at the appointment, printed on the confirmation.

The IND does offer an optional service for EU citizens — a verification against EU community law, €85 for an adult and €46 for a child under 18 — which produces a document confirming lawful residence under EU law. It is not a permit and you do not need it to live or work here. Occasionally an employer, a mortgage lender or a foreign authority asks for documentary proof, which is the only real use case.

**What you gain and lose relative to non-EU colleagues.** You skip the permit fee, the MVV appointment, the sponsor dependency and the integration obligation. You also skip the 30% ruling, in most cases, and that is not a small trade.

## Difference 2: The 150 Kilometre Rule Is the Whole Story

The 30% facility lets a Dutch employer pay part of your salary as a tax-free allowance. It is the single largest financial variable in most international moves to the Netherlands, and the condition that decides it for German movers has nothing to do with your salary or your skills.

**The rule:** you must have lived more than 150 km from the nearest point of the Dutch border for at least 16 of the 24 calendar months before your Dutch employment started. The distance is measured in a straight line from your former address.

**What that means on a German map.** The western half of the country is largely inside the radius — Düsseldorf, Cologne, Dortmund, Frankfurt and parts of Hamburg all fall within 150 km. Berlin, at roughly 570 km, and Munich, at roughly 630 km, are clearly outside. Between those extremes there is a band of addresses where the answer genuinely depends on the street, and there is no official public map you can check informally. If your address is anywhere near the line, get the measurement done professionally before you rely on the outcome.

Two further traps specific to German movers:

- **The 16-of-24 test is cumulative, not continuous.** Eighteen months in Berlin followed by six in Cologne still passes. Twenty months in Cologne followed by four in Berlin fails.
- **Months spent living in the Netherlands count against you.** If you did a Dutch internship or studied here inside that 24-month window, count carefully.

Work through the [30% ruling eligibility guide](/guides/finance/30-percent-ruling-eligibility-guide-2026/) properly, and if the ruling is out, use the [30% ruling calculator guide](/guides/finance/30-percent-ruling-calculator-guide-2026/) to understand what your net salary looks like without it before you sign.

## Difference 3: Your Licence and Your Diplomas Travel

This is where German movers are far better off than the non-EU half of this cluster.

**Driving.** A German licence is an EU licence. There is no exchange obligation and no 185-day cliff. The RDW allows continued use of a foreign EU/EFTA licence in the A, B and BE categories for another 15 years from the date of issue, with C and D categories limited to five years from issue. Exchange is always available if you want it: the RDW can exchange a valid EU/EFTA licence, and unusually it can even exchange an expired one. The practical argument for exchanging voluntarily is renewal — renewing a German licence from a Dutch address is more annoying than holding the Dutch document. See the [licence exchange guide](/guides/daily-life/exchange-driving-license-netherlands-2026/).

**Qualifications.** Regulated professions have an EU recognition route rather than a rebuild. The genuinely awkward case is the German dual system: an Ausbildung or a Meister title maps imperfectly onto Dutch mbo/hbo levels, and Dutch recruiters outside the border provinces often do not know where to place it. If you are in that category, an IDW credential evaluation — a formal comparison to the Dutch education system, available for nationally recognised programmes of at least 1,000 hours — turns an unfamiliar certificate into a Dutch level an HR system can process.

## Difference 4: Health Insurance Switches Systems, Not Just Providers

Your EHIC is a temporary-stay instrument. It covers medically necessary care while you are visiting; it confers nothing in the country where you are resident.

Once you register and work here, you fall under Dutch social insurance law and the basisverzekering becomes compulsory — within four months of your registration date, backdated to that date if you sign up inside the window. Miss it and the CAK sends a warning, then a fine (around €469 in 2026; check the CAK for the current amount), then assigns you a policy.

The structural differences from the German system that catch people out:

- **No employer contribution to your premium in the German sense.** You pay the full nominal premium yourself to a private insurer, with an income-related contribution collected separately through payroll.
- **An annual deductible (eigen risico)** applies to most non-GP care. The GP itself falls outside it.
- **No free choice of specialist.** The huisarts gatekeeps referrals in a way the German system does not.
- **Adult dental care sits outside the basic package.**
- **Lower-income households can claim zorgtoeslag**, a healthcare allowance — see [zorgtoeslag and huurtoeslag](/guides/finance/zorgtoeslag-huurtoeslag-expats-2026/).

The basic package is defined by law and identical at every insurer, so you compare on premium, deductible and service. Read [Dutch health insurance for expats](/guides/health/dutch-health-insurance-guide-expats-2026/), then compare on <a href="https://go.expatnetherlandshub.com/independer-zorg?ref=/guides/legal/moving-to-netherlands-from-germany-2026/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener sponsored">Independer</a> once you have your BSN (affiliate link; we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you).

**The cross-border case.** If you live in the Netherlands but keep working for a German employer, the EU coordination rules — not your address — decide which country insures you and where your social contributions go. That is an assessment to get from the SVB in writing before you cancel any German cover.

## Money: The One Move Where You Do Not Need a Transfer Service

Worth saying plainly, because every relocation guide on the internet will try to sell you a currency account: Germany and the Netherlands are both in the euro, and a SEPA transfer between them is free or near-free and arrives the same day. There is no exchange rate to optimise and no corridor to shop around. If a service quotes you a fee for moving euros from a German account to a Dutch one, you are being sold something you do not need.

Two genuine money items instead:

**Your German IBAN is legally valid here.** Refusing a valid EU IBAN is IBAN discrimination and EU rules prohibit it. But Dutch reality is that iDEAL — the payment rail behind most Dutch webshops, utility portals and government payments — requires a Dutch account, and some payroll and landlord systems still choke on a DE IBAN. Open a Dutch account in your first weeks and keep the German one while it is useful. The [best bank account guide](/guides/finance/best-bank-account-expats-netherlands-2026/) compares the options, including which ones you can open before your BSN arrives.

**A multi-currency account only earns its place if you have non-euro income** — a foreign contract, dividends in another currency, family in a non-eurozone country. If that is you, see the [international money transfer guide](/guides/finance/best-international-money-transfer-netherlands-2026/). If not, skip it.

## The Smaller German-Specific Items

- **Child benefit.** Kindergeld stops with your German deregistration; kinderbijslag from the SVB replaces it and is applied for once you are registered. Do not assume the handover is automatic.
- **Pension.** AOW, the Dutch state pension, accrues by year of residence, so your entitlement builds from your registration date. Insurance periods in other EU states are coordinated under EU rules for the purpose of qualifying periods; ask the DRV and the SVB for a written statement of your position rather than assuming. See the [Dutch pension guide](/guides/finance/dutch-pension-complete-guide-expats-2026/).
- **Rundfunkbeitrag.** It ends with the Abmeldung, not with your departure. Get the deregistration date right.
- **Utilities.** Dutch energy and broadband are contracted separately from the tenancy and are the two things a new arrival most often forgets. ## The Mistake German Movers Make Most Often

Assuming the 30% ruling comes with the job because it came with the job for the colleague sitting next to them. It is the one part of a German move that is decided entirely by an address you have already left, and finding out in month three that your net salary is 15% lower than you modelled is a genuinely bad month. Measure the distance, in writing, before you sign.
