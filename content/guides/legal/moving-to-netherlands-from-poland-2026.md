**Short answer:** three things define a Polish move, and none of them is immigration. There is no permit — but *how* you register (BRP or RNI) determines what you are able to access for years afterwards. Whether you are insured in the Netherlands or remain in Polish social security depends on who employs you and whether an A1 certificate exists, not on where you sleep. And because Poland is in the EU but not the euro, the money corridor is a real cost, unlike moves from Germany or Spain.

Poland is one of the largest source countries for workers in the Netherlands, and the route splits sharply. Specialists arriving on a Dutch employment contract have the easiest move in this cluster. Workers arriving through an employment agency have the same legal rights but far more ways to end up worse off, mostly through registration and housing. This guide covers both.

A Dutch employer, a Dutch employment agency (uitzendbureau) or a Polish employer posting you here are three different legal situations with different consequences for insurance, tax and rights. ## Difference 1: BRP or RNI Is the Decision That Follows You

Every other guide in this cluster treats registration as a formality. For Polish movers it is the single most consequential administrative choice, because two different registers exist and workers frequently end up in the wrong one.

**BRP** is the population register. You are registered as a resident at a Dutch address, legally required within five days of moving in when your stay exceeds four months. It gives you a BSN and, crucially, an address — which is what a bank, an insurer, DigiD, the Belastingdienst allowance system and any landlord will ask for.

**RNI**, the non-residents register, also issues a BSN, but records no Dutch address. It exists for stays under four months: seasonal work, short assignments, cross-border commuting.

The problem in practice is workers who stay for years while remaining RNI-registered, often because an employer or agency finds it simpler. The consequences are quiet but real: difficulty opening a Dutch bank account, difficulty claiming allowances you are entitled to, no DigiD, and no address history when you later want to rent independently or apply for anything. If you are here longer than four months, you belong in the BRP. An employer who actively discourages BRP registration is telling you something about the arrangement.

The IND also offers an optional verification against EU community law at €85 for an adult and €46 for a child under 18. It is a document, not a permit, and most Polish movers never need it.

## Difference 2: Who Insures You — Dutch Employer or A1 Posting

This is the fork that determines your healthcare, your pension accrual and your contributions, and it depends on the employment structure rather than on your address.

**Employed by a Dutch employer or a Dutch agency.** You are normally subject to Dutch social insurance. Dutch basic health insurance is then compulsory within four months of your registration, backdated to the registration date if you sign up inside the window. Miss it and the CAK warns you, fines you (around €469 in 2026 — check the current amount with the CAK) and can assign you a policy. You also start accruing AOW, the Dutch state pension, per year of residence.

**Posted here by a Polish employer.** EU coordination rules can keep you insured in Poland for the duration of the posting, evidenced by an A1 certificate issued in Poland. In that case you do not take out the Dutch basisverzekering for that period, and your contributions continue in the Polish system. The A1 is the document that proves it; without one, you are exposed to being treated as uninsured in the Netherlands.

Because getting this wrong in either direction is expensive — an unnecessary Dutch premium on one side, a CAK fine on the other — confirm your position in writing with the SVB rather than relying on your employer's summary. Read [Dutch health insurance for expats](/guides/health/dutch-health-insurance-guide-expats-2026/) for the Dutch system, and compare premiums via <a href="https://go.expatnetherlandshub.com/independer-zorg?ref=/guides/legal/moving-to-netherlands-from-poland-2026/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener sponsored">Independer</a> once you have a BSN and know you need it.

If you do fall under the Dutch system on a modest income, check zorgtoeslag — a healthcare allowance that offsets part of the premium and that a large number of eligible workers never claim. ## Difference 3: Agency Work, and Housing Tied to Your Contract

A large share of Polish arrivals come through an uitzendbureau. That is entirely legitimate — agency work is a normal part of the Dutch labour market, covered by collective agreements, with a statutory minimum wage and rules on working time. The risk is not the agency model; it is bundling your housing into it.

When the same contract provides your job and your room, ending the assignment ends both on the same day. Legitimate agencies work with certified accommodation standards and are transparent about deductions; the ones to avoid are not.

Ask these questions in writing before you travel, and treat a refusal to answer as the answer:

- What is the notice period on the accommodation, and what happens to it if the assignment ends early?
- Is rent deducted from my wages, at what amount, and is that deduction shown on the payslip?
- May I register at this address in the BRP?
- How many people share the room and the facilities?
- Is the accommodation certified under the Dutch flexible-housing standard?

Also learn to read a Dutch payslip — deductions for housing, transport and insurance are where problems show up first. See [the Dutch payslip explained](/guides/work/dutch-pay-slip-loonstrook-explained-2026/) and [Dutch employment law basics](/guides/legal/dutch-employment-law-basics-expats-2026/), which sets out your rights on contracts, notice and holiday pay regardless of nationality.

## Difference 4: The Zloty Corridor Is a Real Cost

Poland is in the EU but not in the euro area, which puts Polish movers in a different position from German or Spanish ones. A plain SEPA transfer does not make the problem go away, because a euro-to-zloty conversion has to happen somewhere — and where it happens determines what it costs.

Two flows matter, and they run in opposite directions:

**Bringing savings over.** Usually one larger transfer, often needed before you have a Dutch bank account at all. A multi-currency account can be opened from Poland before you leave, without a BSN or Dutch address, giving you a European IBAN to pay a Dutch deposit from.

**Sending money home.** Most Polish workers I speak to send money to Poland regularly — family support, a mortgage, savings kept in zloty. Here the recurring exchange rate spread matters far more than any one-off fee. A bank will typically quote a small fee and take its margin in the rate; the honest comparison is the number of zloty that lands in the Polish account, not the fee on the confirmation screen.

## Licences, Qualifications and Professional Driving

**Driving licence.** Your Polish licence is an EU licence: valid here, no exchange obligation, no 185-day deadline. The RDW permits continued use of an EU/EFTA licence in the A, B and BE categories for another 15 years from the date of issue, but only five years from issue for the C and D categories. If you drive lorries or coaches, that five-year limit and your Code 95 professional competence are the things to diarise — this is the most common way an EU mover accidentally ends up driving without valid entitlement. Voluntary exchange is available at the gemeente; see [exchanging your driving licence](/guides/daily-life/exchange-driving-license-netherlands-2026/).

**Qualifications.** Regulated professions — nursing, medicine, teaching, several technical trades — have an EU recognition route rather than a rebuild, and healthcare additionally requires BIG registration with a Dutch language requirement. Where you need a formal statement of level for a Dutch employer or programme, IDW issues credential evaluations comparing your diploma to the Dutch system, for nationally recognised programmes of at least 1,000 hours, with currently long processing times.

## Tax, Allowances and Pension

Once you are a Dutch tax resident, the Netherlands taxes your worldwide income and the Poland-Netherlands tax treaty allocates taxing rights on Polish-source income such as rental income from a Polish flat. If you are here for part of a year, you will file a partial-year return, and if your family remains in Poland the fiscal-partner and allowance rules become relevant — this is exactly the situation where a cheap error costs more than an adviser. Start with the [Dutch tax system guide](/guides/finance/dutch-tax-system-expats-guide-2026/) and the [tax return guide](/guides/finance/dutch-tax-return-how-to-file-2026/).

On pensions: AOW accrues per year of Dutch residence, and insurance periods completed in other EU states are coordinated under EU rules for qualifying purposes. Ask ZUS and the SVB for written statements of your position rather than assuming either side has the full picture. See the [Dutch pension guide](/guides/finance/dutch-pension-complete-guide-expats-2026/).

Child benefit: kinderbijslag is administered by the SVB, and where children live in another EU state the coordination rules determine which country pays what. Do not assume; ask the SVB.

## The Mistake Polish Movers Make Most Often

Letting someone else handle the registration. Whether you end up in the BRP or the RNI, whether an A1 exists, whether your address can be registered, whether the housing deduction on your payslip is correct — these are all decided in your first weeks, usually by an employer or agency acting in its own interest, and they are much harder to unwind later. Register in the BRP if you are staying longer than four months, get the A1 in your own hands if you are posted, and keep copies of everything.
