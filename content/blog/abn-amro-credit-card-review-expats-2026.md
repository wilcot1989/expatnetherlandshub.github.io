ABN AMRO is one of the three banks most expats end up with, largely because it runs a genuinely usable English-language service and does not treat a foreign passport as a complication. Once the current account is open, the credit card is the obvious next question — it sits one tap away in the app, and it costs a couple of euros a month rather than the eye-watering annual fees some standalone Dutch cards charge.

This review looks at what you actually get for that money, what the card is quietly bad at, and the one structural quirk that confuses nearly every new holder: the bank whose name is on the card is not the company that administers it.

> **Quick answer:** A cheap, unglamorous utility card that makes sense if you already bank with ABN AMRO. Reported at around €2.55 per month in 2026, Mastercard network, administered by ICS rather than by the bank itself, income requirement in the region of €1,500 net per month. Weak point: an exchange rate markup of about 2% on every non-euro transaction, which makes it the wrong card for foreign spending. Pair it with a <a href="https://go.expatnetherlandshub.com/wise?ref=/blog/abn-amro-credit-card-review-expats-2026/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow sponsored noopener">multi-currency account</a> rather than treating it as an all-rounder.

## What the card is

The standard product is called, without much imagination, the ABN AMRO Credit Card. It runs on the Mastercard network, is offered as an add-on to an ABN AMRO payment package, and is settled by direct debit from your ABN AMRO current account. There is a step-up version, the Gold Card, with a higher fee and more insurance cover, and a separate premium line for private banking clients.

It is not a rewards card. There is no points programme worth optimising, no cashback, no sign-up bonus. Dutch banking culture never developed the rewards arms race that shapes credit cards in the US and the UK, largely because EU rules cap the interchange fee that funds those rewards — 0.3% on consumer credit transactions under the Interchange Fee Regulation. There is simply not enough margin in a European credit card to buy your loyalty with air miles.

Understanding that up front reframes the decision. You are not choosing between reward schemes. You are buying a payment instrument that works in the handful of situations a Dutch debit card does not cover.

## The costs, as clearly as they can be stated

ABN AMRO's own tariff pages are the authoritative source, and they are the ones you should read before applying. Independent comparison sites reporting in the first half of 2026 listed the standard card at **€2.55 per month, roughly €30.60 per year**, with a spending limit up to around €5,000 and a minimum net monthly income of about €1,500. Summaries of the Gold Card put its annual cost somewhere in the €50 to €70 range.

The recurring fee is the least interesting number, though. These are the ones that actually move money:

**Exchange rate markup: around 2%.** Applied to any payment or withdrawal in a currency other than the euro. On a €2,000 trip billed in dollars that is €40 you did not notice paying, because it is folded into the converted amount rather than shown as a fee.

**Cash withdrawal: around 4% of the amount, with a minimum charge.** Plus the currency markup if the withdrawal is not in euros. Treat cash advances on this card as an emergency-only function.

**Spread payment interest: 10% effective annually** on ABN AMRO's deferred payment facility, per the ICS service pages that administer it. That is low by international credit card standards — American and British cards routinely charge two or three times as much — but it is still borrowing, and it is the version of the product that gets reported to BKR.

Euro payments in the Netherlands and the wider eurozone carry no transaction fee. If your spending is overwhelmingly domestic, the monthly contribution is the entire cost of ownership.

## The ICS arrangement, explained

New holders regularly contact us confused about the same thing: they applied through ABN AMRO, and then the statements arrive from a company called ICS, on a separate website, with a separate login.

International Card Services is a card specialist founded in 1988 that is part of the ABN AMRO group and holds its own banking licence from De Nederlandsche Bank. It is the largest credit card issuer in the Netherlands and administers card programmes for a range of Dutch brands, ABN AMRO's own included. ABN AMRO sells and brands the card; ICS runs the plumbing.

Practically, this means:

- Your card transactions live in a separate portal and app, not only in the ABN AMRO banking app, although the app does surface a summary.
- Customer service for card disputes, chargebacks, limit changes and fraud goes to ICS, not to your branch.
- Terms and tariffs for the card are set within the ICS-administered programme, which is why the numbers sometimes appear on icscards.nl rather than on abnamro.nl.

None of this is a problem, but knowing it saves you a wasted phone call. We cover the wider picture — including the cards ICS issues under its own name to customers of any bank — in [ICS cards explained](/blog/ics-cards-explained-netherlands-2026/).

## Who can get it

The conditions are the standard Dutch set rather than anything ABN-specific: 18 or over, registered at a Dutch address, an ABN AMRO account for the mainstream route, declared net income above the published threshold, and no arrears code in your BKR file.

There is no requirement to have held credit before, and no penalty for an empty credit register. If that surprises you, the reason is that the Netherlands does not operate a consumer credit score at all — we explain the whole system in [how to build a credit history in the Netherlands](/blog/how-to-build-credit-history-netherlands-expats-2026/), and the application mechanics in [how to get a credit card in the Netherlands](/blog/how-to-get-credit-card-netherlands-2026/).

One point deserves emphasis for anyone thinking about a mortgage in the next few years. The credit **limit**, not your balance, is what a Dutch mortgage adviser deducts from your borrowing capacity, because affordability rules assume a monthly burden on the full facility. A €5,000 limit you never use still costs you house. Ask for the smallest limit that covers your actual needs.

## What it is good at

**Deposits and pre-authorisations.** Car rental desks, some hotels, and a handful of equipment hire companies want a credit card in the driver's or guest's name and will not accept a debit card. This is the single most common reason expats genuinely need one, and the ABN card does the job.

**Online purchases outside the Dutch payment ecosystem.** Dutch e-commerce runs largely on iDEAL, which is a bank transfer rather than a card scheme and does not exist outside the Netherlands. Buying from a German, American or Japanese retailer usually means a card, and a Mastercard is accepted essentially everywhere.

**Chargeback rights.** A card payment carries a dispute mechanism that a bank transfer does not. If a merchant fails to deliver, the card scheme gives you a route to reclaim the money. This is a real, underrated benefit and it applies to the cheap card as much as to the expensive one.

**Integration.** The direct debit settles from the account your salary lands in. No manual top-ups, no forgotten payment, no late fee.

## What it is bad at

**Foreign currency.** The roughly 2% markup is the flaw that matters most for this audience. Expats are, almost by definition, people who spend in more than one currency — visits home, family transfers, subscriptions billed in dollars, flights priced in sterling. Two percent on all of that, invisibly, adds up faster than the €30 annual fee ever will.

**Cash.** A 4% withdrawal charge plus the currency markup is close to punitive. Use your Dutch debit card at an ATM instead.

**Insurance, at the standard tier.** The cheap card is a payment instrument, not a benefits package. If you want travel insurance, purchase protection and car hire excess cover bundled in, you are looking at the Gold tier or at an ICS Gold or Platinum product, and you should compare the premium against what a standalone Dutch travel policy costs. Buying a €175-a-year card for insurance you already hold is a common and expensive mistake.

**Everyday acceptance.** This is not the card's fault, but it bears saying: a credit card is not a general-purpose payment method in the Netherlands. Plenty of supermarkets, market stalls, bakeries and small shops take debit only. Your Dutch debit card remains your primary instrument, and the credit card is the specialist tool. If that seems strange, [we explain why Dutch retailers behave this way](/blog/why-dutch-stores-refuse-foreign-credit-cards-2026/).

## The pairing that actually works

The sensible setup for most expats is not "which card should I choose" but "which two".

Keep the ABN AMRO card for euro-denominated situations where a credit card is required — deposits, pre-authorisations, chargeback protection on a large purchase. It costs a few euros a month and asks nothing of you.

Then keep everything with a currency conversion in it somewhere cheaper. ## Alternatives worth comparing

**ING or Rabobank credit card**, if you bank there instead. Same category of product, comparable fees, same integration argument. Our [ING versus Rabobank piece](/blog/ing-vs-rabobank-expats-2026/) covers the accounts; the cards follow the accounts.

**ICS Visa or Mastercard directly**, if you want more insurance or you bank somewhere without a card offering. Available to holders of any Dutch bank account.

**American Express**, if you value the service and protections and can live with narrower Dutch acceptance.

**bunq**, if you want a Dutch-licensed account and card from a mobile-first provider. Our [bunq review](/guides/finance/bunq-review-expats-netherlands-2026/) has the detail.

**No credit card at all.** Genuinely an option. If you never rent cars and never hit a pre-authorisation, a Dutch debit card plus a multi-currency account covers your entire life here.

## The verdict

As a card, unremarkable. As a decision, easy.

If you bank with ABN AMRO, the credit card is cheap enough that the question is less "is it good value" than "will I ever need a credit card at all". If the answer is yes — and for most people it is, once a year, at a rental counter — then paying roughly €30 a year for the capability is reasonable, and the integration with an account you already hold removes all the friction.

Just do not make it your travel card. The currency markup is where this product quietly earns back its low headline fee, and it is entirely avoidable.
