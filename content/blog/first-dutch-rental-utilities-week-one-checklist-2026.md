The first week in a Dutch rental has a particular texture: boxes everywhere, no internet, a meter cupboard you do not understand, and a growing pile of Dutch-language post addressed to someone who moved out three years ago. Almost everything on that pile has a deadline, and two of them are short.

The good news is that the whole utilities set-up is genuinely a one-week job if you do it in the right order. The bad news is that the order matters more than the effort. Register with the gemeente late and your BSN, health insurance and bank account all slip. Apply for internet late and no amount of urgency compresses a fibre installation. Fail to photograph the meters on day one and you have no defence against a bill for the previous tenant's consumption.

This is that week, in order, with the specific Dutch details that trip up new arrivals. For the broader background on how each utility works in the Netherlands, see [Dutch utilities explained for expats](/blog/dutch-utilities-explained-expats-2026/) — this piece is the practical sequence rather than the explainer.

## Before the Keys: Two Things Worth Doing Early

If you are reading this before you move in, two items are worth pulling forward.

**Book the gemeente appointment now.** In the larger cities — Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht, Eindhoven — registration appointments are booked weeks ahead and walk-ins are not accommodated. You can usually report a move within the Netherlands up to four weeks before the date, which means the appointment can be in the diary before you have unpacked anything.

**Apply for internet as soon as you have a signed contract and an address.** You do not need to have moved in. Application costs nothing, and the connection date is set by the network operator, not by your urgency.

Everything else genuinely can wait until you have keys.

## Day 1: Meters, Photographs, and What Is Already Running

### Photograph everything before you unpack a single box

Find the meter cupboard (*meterkast*) — usually by the front door, in a hallway, or in a shared stairwell for apartments. Photograph:

- **The electricity meter**, including *both* registers if it has a day and night tariff (marked something like 1.8.1 and 1.8.2, or *normaal* and *dal*).
- **The gas meter**, if you have gas. Newer builds increasingly do not.
- **The water meter**.
- **The serial number** on each meter, which is how a supplier identifies your specific connection.
- The **EAN codes** for electricity and gas if they are shown in the cupboard — eighteen-digit numbers unique to your connection. If they are not posted, your energy supplier can look them up from your address.

Make sure the photos carry a date. This set of images is the single cheapest insurance policy of the whole move: if a supplier later opens your account with an estimated starting reading that is too low, or the previous occupant disputes the closing reading, these photographs settle it.

### Establish what is already supplied

Three possibilities, and it matters which one you are in:

1. **Utilities are included in the rent.** Common in serviced apartments, short-stay housing and some student accommodation. Check the contract for what "included" covers and whether there is a fair-use cap above which you are billed. Also check whether the landlord settles an annual reconciliation — if so, your meter photos matter for that too.
2. **Nothing is connected and you must arrange everything.** Standard for ordinary long-term rentals.
3. **A supply is still running in the previous occupant's name.** This is the situation to sort out immediately. You are consuming energy that is being billed to someone else's account, and the supplier will eventually reconcile it. Contact a supplier and register from your move-in date with your day-one readings.

There is no risk of the lights going off while you sort this out — the Netherlands has a supplier-of-last-resort arrangement, so a property is not simply disconnected. But that fallback is not a cheap tariff, and it is not a reason to delay.

### Check the heating and hot water

Find out whether you have a *combiketel* (combination boiler doing both heating and hot water), district heating (*stadswarmte* or *warmtenet*), or a heat pump. This changes your bills substantially: district heating is billed by a single regional supplier under regulated tariffs rather than through the competitive energy market, so if your home is on a heat network there is nothing to compare for heating — only electricity.

## Day 1–2: Register With the Gemeente

You must report your address to the municipality **within five days of moving in**. This is not a bureaucratic nicety — the *Basisregistratie Personen* (BRP) entry at your address is what triggers your BSN if you are new to the country, and what allows almost everything else: a bank account, health insurance, a GP registration, and the automatic assessment of municipal charges.

Bring your passport or ID, your rental contract or a landlord's declaration, and your birth certificate (legalised and translated where required) if this is your first registration in the Netherlands.

If you are new to the country rather than moving within it, the full sequence is set out in [registering with the gemeente and getting your BSN](/guides/legal/bsn-registration-guide-2026/) and the [first 30 days checklist](/guides/legal/first-30-days-netherlands-expat-checklist-2026/).

## Day 2: Internet — The Longest Lead Time on the List

Apply for internet before you arrange anything else that is optional, because it is the item where waiting has the largest cost.

**What determines the timeline:**

- **An existing, active connection you can self-install** — a router posted to you, plugged into an existing socket. Days.
- **An existing connection that needs activation or an engineer visit** — one to a few weeks, depending on engineer availability.
- **A new fibre connection where none exists** — this can run to several weeks, because it involves the network operator, not just your provider.

**What to check before choosing a package:**

- **Which networks reach your address.** Fibre coverage is extensive in the Netherlands but not universal, and coaxial cable availability differs by neighbourhood. Providers check this by postcode and house number.
- **Whether the building already has a connection.** In apartment buildings, ask the landlord or the VvE (owners' association) what is installed. It changes the install type entirely.
- **Contract term.** One-year terms are standard; longer terms buy discounts you may not get to use if your stay is uncertain.
- **English-language service.** Not all providers handle account changes and faults comfortably in English. If your Dutch is limited, this is a real selection criterion, not a nice-to-have.
- **Bundles.** Internet with TV and mobile is often cheaper than separate contracts, but only if you actually want the TV component. Dutch broadcast TV is of limited use to most internationals.

## Day 2–3: Energy

Energy is a genuinely competitive market in the Netherlands. You choose your supplier freely; the *netbeheerder* (network operator — Liander, Stedin, Enexis and others) is determined by your region and is not something you select. The network charges appear on your bill regardless of supplier and are the same for everyone in that region.

### Fixed, variable or dynamic

- **Fixed (vast).** The per-kWh and per-m³ rates are locked for the contract term. Predictable, easy to budget, and the usual choice for people who want to stop thinking about it.
- **Variable (variabel).** Rates change periodically — typically twice a year — and you can cancel at short notice without penalty.
- **Dynamic (dynamisch).** Hourly wholesale-linked pricing. Genuinely cheaper for households that can shift consumption — EV charging overnight, running the washing machine at cheap hours — and genuinely worse for households that cannot. Not a first-week decision; it needs a smart meter and some attention.

### The moving-house right you should know about

**Moving home gives you the right to end an energy contract early without an exit fee**, regardless of how much of the term remains. This is worth knowing for two reasons: it means a fixed contract does not trap you if your Dutch address changes, and it means that when you eventually move again, you are not stuck.

Note the distinction: some suppliers let you *take the contract with you* to the new address, which is often the simplest option if you are happy with the rates. Others treat it as a termination and a new contract. Ask which, because taking a favourable old contract to a new address can be worth more than any switching bonus.

### What your bill is actually made of

Your annual energy cost is consumption charges, network operator charges, energy tax (*energiebelasting*) including the standard reduction applied to every connection, and VAT. Only part of it is competitive, which is why the difference between the cheapest and most expensive supplier is smaller than headline comparisons sometimes suggest — but it is still real money over a year.

### Set the monthly instalment realistically

You pay a monthly *termijnbedrag* (estimated instalment) and receive an annual reconciliation (*jaarafrekening*). Suppliers estimate your consumption from household size and property type. For a new arrival with no consumption history, the estimate is a guess. Two consequences:

- Set too low, and the annual reconciliation lands as a large single bill.
- Set too high, and you are lending the supplier money interest-free for a year.

Check it after two or three months against your actual meter readings and adjust. Almost all suppliers let you change the instalment in their app.

## Day 2–3: Water (No Choice to Make)

Drinking water in the Netherlands is supplied by regional companies with no competition. Which one serves you depends on your postcode — Waternet in the Amsterdam region, Vitens across much of the centre and east, Evides in the south-west, PWN in North Holland, Brabant Water, Dunea, and others.

There is therefore nothing to compare. You just register. Find your supplier by postcode on the company's site or through the sector body's postcode tool, then register your move with your day-one meter reading.

Billing works like energy: a monthly or quarterly instalment against an annual reconciliation. Water is a small line in a Dutch household budget compared with energy, and the main thing to get right is registering promptly so the starting reading is yours and not inherited.

**Do not confuse the water company with the water board.** *Waterschapsbelasting* is a tax levied by the regional water authority for flood defence and water-level management, billed separately from your drinking water. Which brings us to the post.

## Day 3–5: The Post, the Taxes, and the Bins

### Municipal and water board charges

Once you are registered at the address, assessments arrive automatically. As a **tenant**, expect:

- **Afvalstoffenheffing** — the waste collection levy. Billed to the occupant. In many municipalities the amount depends on household size, and some use a variable component based on how much residual waste you put out.
- **Rioolheffing** — sewerage charge. Depending on the municipality this falls on the occupant, the owner, or is split.
- **Waterschapsbelasting** — the water board's occupant charge (*watersysteemheffing ingezetenen* and a purification levy).

**OZB** (property tax) is the owner's charge and should not appear on your bill as a tenant. If it does, query it.

These assessments typically go out in the first months of the calendar year, are often combinable into monthly instalments by direct debit, and most municipalities have a *kwijtschelding* (waiver) route for low incomes. If you arrive mid-year, the charges are usually apportioned from your registration date.

### Waste separation

Dutch waste separation is stricter than many arrivals expect and varies by municipality: GFT (organic), PMD (plastic, metal, drink cartons), paper, glass, textiles, and residual. Some areas collect at the kerb on a schedule; others use neighbourhood underground containers that need a resident pass. Check the gemeente's site or app for your street's schedule and whether you need a container card — and get one early, because an unopenable container with a full bag is a genuinely annoying first-week problem.

### Read all the post

Post addressed to previous occupants can be returned or discarded, but read anything addressed to "de bewoners van" (the occupants of) — it includes container passes, meter-reading requests, municipal charge notices, and network operator notifications. Dutch institutions still rely on paper for things that matter.

## Day 3–7: Insurance and the Things That Are Easy to Forget

### Contents and liability insurance

Your landlord's building insurance covers the building. It does not cover your laptop, your bike, your furniture or your deposit-relevant damage.

- **Inboedelverzekering** (contents) covers your possessions against fire, theft, water damage and similar.
- **Aansprakelijkheidsverzekering particulieren (AVP)** covers damage you cause to other people or their property — the one that matters most in a country where you will be cycling daily and living in a building where a burst hose affects the flat below. Many letting agents expect tenants to hold it.

They are commonly sold together and are inexpensive relative to the exposure. ### Health insurance is the hard deadline

If you are new to the Netherlands and required to be insured, Dutch basic health insurance (*basisverzekering*) must be arranged within four months of becoming insurable, with cover backdated to that date. This is the deadline with the largest financial penalty attached, and it is entirely separate from utilities. See [best Dutch health insurance plans](/blog/best-dutch-health-insurance-plans-2026/).

### The rest of week one

- **Register with a GP (huisarts).** Practices in popular city neighbourhoods regularly have closed patient lists. Start calling in week one, not when you are ill.
- **Set up direct debits (automatische incasso).** Dutch billing assumes direct debit. Doing it once removes a category of problem.
- **Note your rental inspection findings in writing.** Photograph existing damage and email the list to the landlord in the first days. It is your deposit.
- **Check the smoke alarms.** Required in Dutch homes; test them rather than assuming.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I do on the actual day I get the keys?

Photograph every meter with a timestamp — electricity (both registers if applicable), gas, water — plus the meter serial numbers and any visible EAN codes. Then find out whether any supply is still running in the previous occupant's name and register from your move-in date.

### Can I choose my own water company?

No. Drinking water is a regional monopoly determined by postcode, so there is nothing to compare — only to register. Energy and internet are competitive and worth comparing.

### How long does internet installation take?

Days if there is an active connection you can self-install; several weeks if a new fibre connection or an engineer visit is needed. Apply as soon as you have an address, and bridge the gap with a data-heavy prepaid SIM as a hotspot.

### Do I pay municipal and water board taxes as a tenant?

Yes, for the occupant-based charges — the waste levy and the water board's occupant charge — and sometimes the sewerage charge depending on the municipality. Property tax (OZB) is the owner's. Assessments arrive automatically once you are registered at the address.

### Should I take a fixed or variable energy contract?

Fixed for predictability, variable for flexibility, dynamic only if you can genuinely shift your consumption to cheap hours. Moving home lets you end an energy contract early without an exit fee, so a fixed term does not lock you to an address.

### Is contents insurance necessary in a rented flat?

Not legally, but your landlord's policy covers the building rather than your belongings. Contents cover plus personal liability cover (AVP) is inexpensive and is what letting agents commonly expect tenants to hold.

## Final Thoughts

The whole utilities set-up is perhaps four hours of actual work spread over a week. The reason it goes wrong is almost never effort — it is sequence. Meters get photographed after the boxes are unpacked and half the readings are already unverifiable. Internet gets applied for in week three and arrives in week seven. The gemeente appointment gets booked after arrival and lands three weeks out, delaying the BSN and everything downstream.

Do the two long-lead items first (gemeente appointment, internet application), the two day-one items immediately (meter photos, existing-supply check), and then work through energy, water, insurance and the post at a civilised pace.
