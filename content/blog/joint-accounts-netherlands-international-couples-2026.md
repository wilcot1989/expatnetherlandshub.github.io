Almost every international couple who moves here asks the same question in the first month, and it is usually the wrong one. The question is "which bank does joint accounts?" — and the answer is all of them, which tells you nothing useful.

The questions that actually matter are different. Who can spend the money without asking? Does putting both names on an account change who owns it? What happens to it if one of you dies, or if you separate? And what do you do in the six weeks where one partner has a BSN and the other does not?

Those have concrete Dutch answers, and they are not the same as the answers in most countries people arrive from.

> **Quick answer:** The Dutch default is an en/of rekening — either holder can use the whole balance independently, with no second signature required. All three big banks offer them, and all three require both holders to have a BSN and a Dutch registration, which is the real bottleneck for couples who arrive separately. A joint account grants authority, not ownership: who the money belongs to is set by your matrimonial regime or agreement, not by the account. Deposit protection is per person, so two holders can mean €200,000. The surviving holder normally keeps access if one of you dies.

## En/of Versus En: The Distinction Nobody Explains

Dutch banks offer joint accounts in two legal shapes, and the naming is easy to skim past.

**En/of rekening ("and/or account").** Either holder can act alone. Withdrawals, transfers, standing orders, direct debit mandates — one signature is enough, and neither of you needs the other's permission. This is what you get by default when you ask a Dutch bank for a gezamenlijke rekening, and it is what the overwhelming majority of couples here use.

**En rekening ("and account").** Both holders must act together for every transaction. It is far less common in consumer banking and is mostly encountered in specific contexts such as estates or where two parties deliberately want mutual control.

The practical consequence of the default is worth stating plainly, because it is the thing couples wish they had understood earlier: on an en/of rekening, your partner can move the entire balance without telling you, and the bank has no basis to stop them. That is not a loophole. It is the structure functioning as designed. Whether that is acceptable depends on your relationship and on what you keep in the account — which is why the account structure and the savings structure should be decided separately.

## Who Offers Joint Accounts, and What They Require

**ING, ABN AMRO and Rabobank** all offer joint accounts to two holders. In each case both people are verified individually, which means each of you needs:

- A valid passport or EU/EEA identity card
- A BSN
- Registration in the Dutch population register (BRP), with proof of your registered address

The addresses do not have to be the same one, but the registrations do have to exist. ## Authority Is Not Ownership

This is the single most misunderstood point, and it has real financial consequences.

Putting both names on an account gives you both **authority** over the balance. It does not settle **ownership**. Who the money belongs to is determined elsewhere:

**If you married in the Netherlands on or after 1 January 2018**, the default regime is the limited community of property (beperkte gemeenschap van goederen). Assets you each brought into the marriage stay individual, as do inheritances and gifts; what you build during the marriage is shared. This was a significant change from the older unlimited regime, and couples who assume the pre-2018 rules get it wrong in both directions.

**If you married before that, or abroad**, which regime applies is a question of law that depends on when and where you married and what you agreed. Cross-border marriages are precisely the case where this gets complicated, and it is worth an hour with a notary rather than a guess.

**If you have huwelijkse voorwaarden** (a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement), that document governs, and the joint account does not override it.

**If you are unmarried and have no cohabitation agreement**, there is no shared property regime at all. Each of you owns what you contributed. A joint account does not create shared ownership, and it does not create the legal protections that a samenlevingscontract or registered partnership would.

That last case is where unmarried international couples take on risk without noticing. If one partner funds a joint account substantially more than the other over years, the money is not automatically half the other's — and, going the other way, sustained transfers of significant sums from one unmarried partner to the other can attract gift tax attention, since the exemption between unmarried partners without a qualifying arrangement is low. Ordinary shared household spending is not the concern here; large asset movements are. If the amounts are meaningful, ask a Dutch notary or tax adviser, not your bank.

**On the tax-status question specifically:** a joint account does not make you fiscal partners. Fiscal partnership follows marriage or registered partnership, or — for unmarried couples registered at the same address — a defined list of criteria such as a notarial cohabitation agreement, a shared child, joint ownership of your home, or naming each other as pension partner. A shared bank account is not on that list, so opening one does not change your tax filing position.

## Deposit Protection: Two Names, Two Limits

The Dutch deposit guarantee scheme covers eligible deposits up to €100,000 per person, per banking licence, and it treats joint account holders separately.

So a joint account held by two people, where neither holds anything else at that bank, is protected up to €200,000. That is a genuine advantage of joint holding for couples with substantial savings.

The qualifier catches people out. Your share of the joint account is added to your personal deposits at the same licence. If you hold €160,000 jointly, attributed €80,000 each, and you also hold €40,000 in your own name at that bank, your individual total is €120,000 — €20,000 of it unguaranteed. Your partner, at €80,000, is still comfortably inside their limit.

The other trap is licences rather than brands: several Dutch names sit under one banking licence and share one limit. ## Death and Separation

Two situations nobody wants to plan for, and both are materially affected by the account structure.

**If one holder dies.** A single-name Dutch account is typically blocked on death until the heirs establish entitlement, sometimes requiring a certificate of inheritance from a notary. A joint account behaves differently: the surviving co-holder generally keeps access and can continue using the account once the death is reported, and the card stays valid. Banks attach their own conditions to that continuity, so confirm the process with yours rather than relying on a general rule.

Critically, retained access is not the same as ownership. The deceased's share of the balance forms part of the estate and is relevant for Dutch inheritance tax. Using the account freely afterwards does not make the money yours, and it can complicate the estate administration. For couples with meaningful assets, this belongs in an estate conversation — see our overview of [Dutch inheritance tax for expats](/guides/finance/dutch-inheritance-tax-expats-2026/).

**If you separate.** Either holder of an en/of rekening can empty it, and there is no notice period and no bank veto. This is the structural risk of the default account type, and the practical mitigation is simply not to hold your savings there. Notify the bank early in a separation, because both of you retain full authority until the account is formally changed.

## Three Structures That Work

**The full joint model.** One en/of account, both salaries in, everything out of it. Simple, and it suits couples with fully merged finances and comparable incomes. It offers no protection against unilateral withdrawal and no privacy, and it makes any later disentangling harder.

**The hybrid three-account model.** Two individual accounts plus one joint account for shared costs. Each of you transfers an agreed amount into the joint account monthly; rent, energy, insurance and groceries are paid from it. Savings stay individual, or go to a clearly designated savings account. This is the structure most couples end up at, and it is the one I would suggest to a couple arriving now — it gives shared administration without shared exposure.

**Separate accounts with settling up.** No joint account at all; one of you pays a given bill and requests the share back, typically through a payment request. It works, it keeps everything clean, and it becomes tedious once you share a household with recurring direct debits. Reasonable early on, or for couples who deliberately keep finances separate.

Whichever you choose, get the direct debits pointed at the right account from the start. Health insurance, energy, internet and the municipal taxes all collect automatically here, and moving mandates later is more administration than setting them up correctly once. If you do need to move everything, the banks operate a switching service — see our walkthrough of the [Overstapservice](/blog/overstapservice-bank-switching-netherlands-expats-2026/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is an en/of rekening?**
A joint account in two names where either holder can use the whole balance independently. The alternative en-rekening requires both holders to act together and is uncommon in consumer banking.

**Do both of us need a BSN?**
At ING, ABN AMRO and Rabobank, yes — each holder is verified against the Dutch population register, so both need a BSN and a Dutch registration.

**Does a joint account mean we own half each?**
No. It grants authority over the balance. Ownership follows your matrimonial regime, prenuptial or cohabitation agreement, or contributions.

**Are we covered twice by the deposit guarantee?**
Each holder is covered separately, so up to €200,000 in total — but your share is added to your personal deposits at the same banking licence against your own €100,000 limit.

**What happens if one of us dies?**
The surviving co-holder normally keeps access and the card stays valid after notifying the bank. The deceased's share still belongs to the estate and matters for inheritance tax.

**Can my partner empty the account?**
On an en/of rekening, yes. That is why the common recommendation is to keep the joint account for shared costs and savings elsewhere.

**Does a joint account make us fiscal partners?**
No. Fiscal partnership depends on marriage, registered partnership, or specific criteria for unmarried couples at the same address. A shared account is not one of them.

**Can we open a joint account before both of us have arrived?**
Not at a traditional Dutch bank. Give the second partner an individual euro IBAN in the meantime and use shared-budget features until both BSNs exist.

## The Short Version

Ask for an en/of rekening and understand what you are getting: either of you can spend all of it, at any time, without asking.

Both of you need a BSN, so plan for the gap if you arrive separately — an individual euro IBAN for the second partner solves the first six weeks. Do not treat the account as settling ownership; if the sums are significant or you are unmarried, get the underlying agreement right with a notary. And take the €200,000 deposit protection into account if you hold real savings, while remembering it is counted per person and per licence, not per account.

My practical tip: use the three-account model. One joint account funded monthly for the household, two individual accounts behind it. It survives a good relationship, a difficult month and a Dutch bank's paperwork equally well.
