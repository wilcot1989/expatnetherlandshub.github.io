When I moved to the Netherlands five years ago, I genuinely thought I had it under control. I had a spreadsheet, two binders, a colour-coded calendar, and what I believed was a generous four-week head start. By the time I landed at Schiphol I was already two weeks behind, missing a document I did not know I needed, and sleeping in a hostel in Amsterdam Noord while frantically refreshing housing platforms on hotel Wi‑Fi.

This guide is the one I wish someone had handed me before I started. It walks through the three real phases of moving here — the four weeks before you arrive, the first week on the ground, and the first month after — with the actual tasks, the order I would do them in, and the tools I now use to keep everything from collapsing.

> **Quick answer:** Plan your Dutch move in three phases. Pre-arrival (4+ weeks out): MVV/visa, document apostille, housing search, BSN appointment booking, shipping quotes, travel insurance. Arrival week: gemeente registration for your BSN, opening a Dutch bank account, OV‑chipkaart, local SIM. First month: DigiD activation, mandatory health insurance, 30% ruling via your employer, energy and internet contracts, GP registration, and your first dealings with the Belastingdienst. Use a structured tool like [verhuisplanner.app](https://verhuisplanner.app) to keep all 50+ tasks on a single timeline instead of in your head.

## What I got wrong on my own move

I want to start with the messy part, because every "ultimate guide" I read before moving made the process sound much tidier than it is.

The first mistake I made was assuming my birth certificate from home was good enough as-is. It was not. The gemeente in Utrecht would not accept it without an apostille from my country of origin, and the apostille office back home had a 17 working‑day queue. I lost two full weeks just on that, which pushed back my BSN appointment, which pushed back opening a bank account, which pushed back signing the lease on a flat I had already paid a holding deposit for. The landlord was patient. I was lucky.

The second mistake was underestimating volume. I thought "moving abroad" was maybe 15 to 20 tasks. By the time I had a working life here it was closer to 70. Some were tiny (buying a SIM card, getting a fietsverlichting set so I would not get fined at night) and some were huge (the 30% ruling application, which my employer had to file for me), but every single one had a deadline attached to something else.

The third mistake was assuming I would "figure it out when I got there." The Dutch system rewards people who book appointments in advance. The gemeente, the IND, the Belastingdienst, even some banks — they all run on appointment slots that fill up two to three weeks ahead. If you arrive without anything booked, you wait.

That is what this guide is really trying to prevent.

## Phase 1: Pre-arrival (4+ weeks before)

This is the phase where most people either do too little or panic and do everything in the wrong order. Here is the order I would use today.

### 1. Visa and residence permit (MVV/IND)

If you are a non-EU citizen, your employer or sponsor will usually start the MVV (Machtiging tot Voorlopig Verblijf) process for you through the [IND](https://ind.nl). Confirm in writing which steps are theirs and which are yours. Ask specifically: who books the biometrics appointment, who picks up the residence permit card, and what the reference number is. I have seen too many people assume HR is handling something HR thinks they are handling themselves.

EU citizens can skip the MVV but still need to register at the gemeente within five days of arriving if they intend to stay longer than four months. More on that in the arrival section, and in my [BSN number guide](/guides/legal/bsn-registration-guide-2026/).

### 2. Document apostille and translation

Get apostilles on:

- Your birth certificate
- Your marriage certificate (if applicable)
- Any diploma you want recognised for professional purposes
- A police clearance certificate if your employer or sector requires one

If your documents are not in English, Dutch, German, or French, you will also need a sworn translation. Both steps take time. Both have to be done from your country of origin, which is much harder once you are already living in Amsterdam.

I should have started this on day one. I started it on day twelve. Do not be me.

### 3. Housing search

Housing is the single most stressful pre-arrival task, especially in Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Rotterdam. I now tell every client the same thing: do not try to sign a long-term lease before you arrive. You cannot view the property properly, you cannot verify the landlord, and you cannot register at the gemeente without a real rental contract that includes registration permission.

Instead, book a short-term furnished place for the first four to eight weeks. That gives you a registered address (if it allows registration), a quiet base to do the rest of your admin, and time to view long-term rentals in person.

### 4. Book your BSN/gemeente appointment

The BSN (Burgerservicenummer) is your Dutch tax ID, and you get it by registering as a resident at the gemeente (city hall) of the municipality where you will live. Most large cities open appointment slots two to four weeks in advance. Book the slot as soon as you have a confirmed arrival date and a temporary address.

If you are moving to Amsterdam, The Hague, Rotterdam, Utrecht, Eindhoven, or Groningen, there is also an "expat centre" that handles IND and gemeente registration in a single appointment. Book through the gemeente website, not via third parties.

### 5. Shipping quotes and what to actually bring

Get at least three quotes from international movers. Ask each one specifically for:

- Door-to-door pricing
- Customs paperwork handling (especially important for non-EU origin)
- Insurance per cubic metre
- Storage fees if your Dutch flat is not ready on arrival

For most clients I work with, shipping a full container makes financial sense only if you have meaningful furniture or you are coming from a country where replacements would cost more than the freight. Otherwise, sell, donate, and rebuild on arrival. Dutch flats are smaller than you expect.

You will not be on Dutch health insurance from day one. There is a legal gap between arrival and when you can enrol, and during that gap you need real cover, not just a credit card travel policy.

## Phase 2: Arrival week

The first week is heavier than it looks on paper. Plan to do almost nothing else.

### Day 1: Airport pickup and temporary stay

Schiphol to anywhere on the Randstad is straightforward by train (NS Intercity), but if you are arriving with two suitcases, a partner, and possibly a cat, book a fixed-price taxi or Uber in advance. I once tried to wrestle three bags onto the train at rush hour and I do not recommend it.

Check into your short-stay. Sleep. Buy a stroopwafel. Resist the urge to do admin on day one.

### Day 2–3: SIM card and OV-chipkaart

Get a prepaid Dutch SIM (Lebara, Lyca, or Simyo are easy starters; switch to a contract later once you have a BSN and a bank account). You need a Dutch number for almost every appointment confirmation that follows.

Buy an anonymous OV-chipkaart at any train station for €7.50, or set up OVpay on your contactless bank card if it is supported. This is your public transport access for trains, trams, metros, and buses across the country.

### Day 3–4: BSN registration at the gemeente

This is the appointment you booked back in phase 1. Bring:

- Your passport
- Your rental contract or short-stay confirmation (with registration permission)
- Your apostilled and translated birth certificate
- Your marriage certificate if applicable
- Your MVV/residence permit sticker if non-EU

The appointment itself usually takes 20–30 minutes. Your BSN is often handed to you on the spot or arrives by post within five working days. From the moment you have it, the rest of the country opens up.

### Day 5: Open a Dutch bank account

With a BSN in hand, you can open a real Dutch bank account. The big three (ING, ABN AMRO, Rabobank) all have English-language onboarding now, but the friction varies. For most expats I recommend Bunq because the account opens in about ten minutes from your phone and accepts non-resident applicants with a BSN.

Use the weekend (or your first quiet day) to:

- Email your employer your BSN so payroll can start

- Set up iDEAL on your new Dutch bank account (you need this for almost every Dutch website)
- Save your gemeente registration confirmation as a PDF — you will be asked for it three or four more times in the next month

## Phase 3: Post-arrival (first month)

This is the phase where it feels like things are calming down, and that is exactly when expats miss the deadlines that matter. Two of them are non-negotiable.

### 1. Activate DigiD

DigiD is the Dutch government's single-sign-on for almost every official service: tax filings, health insurance enrolment, GP portals, gemeente services, parking permits. You apply online with your BSN, then a letter with an activation code arrives at your registered address within five working days.

Do not let this letter sit unopened. The code expires. I cover the activation steps in my [DigiD guide](/guides/legal/digid-guide-expats-2026/).

### 2. Health insurance (mandatory within 4 months)

This is the deadline I almost missed. Every resident of the Netherlands is legally required to take out a basic Dutch health insurance policy (basisverzekering) within four months of registering at the gemeente. The cover then backdates to your registration date, and you pay the missed months retroactively.

If you do not enrol, the CAK steps in, signs you up automatically, and adds a fine of around 130% of the monthly premium per month of delay. It is not worth the gamble.

Compare basisverzekering policies through Zorgwijzer or Independer once you have your BSN. Premiums in 2026 hover around €145–€170 per month for basic cover. My deeper breakdown is in my [Dutch health insurance guide for expats](/guides/health/dutch-health-insurance-guide-expats-2026/).

### 3. 30% ruling application

If you are a highly skilled migrant earning above the salary threshold, you may qualify for the 30% ruling, which lets your employer pay 30% of your gross salary tax-free as a reimbursement for the costs of moving abroad. The rules changed in 2024 and tightened again in 2026, so the headline number is now lower and tapers over time, but it is still worth tens of thousands of euros over five years for most qualifying expats.

Your employer files the application with the Belastingdienst. You provide the supporting documents. File it within four months of starting work and the ruling applies from your first working day. File it later and you lose the back-period. Full eligibility detail is in my [30% ruling guide](/guides/finance/30-percent-ruling-netherlands-2026/).

### 4. Energy and internet

Once you sign a long-term rental, you usually choose your own energy supplier (electricity and gas) and internet provider. If the previous tenant left an active contract, you have 30 days to switch or take over.

For energy, the cheapest contracts in 2026 are still variable-tariff with Vandebron, Eneco, or Greenchoice. For internet, KPN, Odido, and Ziggo dominate; in apartments with fibre, you usually get a better deal through a smaller reseller like Freedom Internet.

This is the kind of detail [verhuisplanner.app](https://verhuisplanner.app) flags automatically based on your postcode, which saves me from having to look up which providers actually cover each street.

### 5. Tax obligations and the Belastingdienst

The Dutch tax year is the calendar year. If you arrive partway through the year, you will file an "M-form" (migration form) for that year, which splits the year into pre- and post-arrival income. The [Belastingdienst](https://www.belastingdienst.nl) sends this form automatically once your registration shows up in the system, usually in spring of the following year. Do not file a normal P-form by mistake — it will under- or overstate your worldwide income.

If you have savings, investments, or property abroad, also read up on Box 3 obligations. Many expats forget that the Dutch system taxes wealth, not just income.

### 6. Register with a GP (huisarts)

The Dutch system runs on the huisarts (general practitioner). You cannot self-refer to a specialist; the huisarts is your gateway. Practices in cities fill up fast, so the moment you have an address, search "huisarts inschrijven [your postcode]" and start calling. Some practices have waiting lists of months. Some will only register you if you live within a defined catchment area.

Pharmacies (apotheken) also work on registration: you tie yourself to one nearby pharmacy and they hold your prescription history.

## Putting it all together

If you take only one thing from this guide, take this: the order matters more than the speed. You cannot do step 7 before step 4, and trying to compress the calendar by skipping ahead is what causes the avoidable failures.

Build a single timeline. Put the IND, gemeente, and health insurance deadlines on it first. Then everything else slots around those. Whether you use a spreadsheet, a Notion board, or [verhuisplanner.app](https://verhuisplanner.app), the format matters less than the discipline of having one source of truth.

And once you arrive, give yourself permission to do less than you planned for the first three days. The country is not going anywhere. You will get through your list. The expats who burn out in month one are almost always the ones who tried to finish in week one.
