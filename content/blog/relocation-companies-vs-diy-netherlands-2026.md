I moved to the Netherlands by myself, without a relocation company, and I survived it. I also paid for that decision in months of my life I will never get back. Housing alone took me four months of evening searches, twelve viewings, two rejected applications, and a temporary stay in a friend's spare room in Haarlem that I had not planned for. If someone had handed me a €2,000 housing-search package on day one, I would have taken it without thinking twice.

A year later I was coaching a software engineer from Bangalore through her move to Eindhoven. Her employer offered a €6,500 relocation budget through Expat Management Group. We sat down with the quote, broke it apart line by line, and worked out what was genuinely worth paying for and what she could absorb herself. The answer was not "all of it" and it was not "none of it" — and that is the question this guide tries to answer for you.

## What a Relocation Company Actually Does

The term "relocation company" gets used loosely. A moving company shifts your physical belongings; a relocation company manages the human side of the move. The four big Dutch and Dutch-active players I see in client quotes are:

- **Expat Management Group (EMG)** — Amsterdam-based, strong on corporate accounts and 30% ruling applications.
- **Crown Relocations** — global brand with a Hoofddorp office, often bundled with their international moving arm.
- **Erasmus Relocation Services** — Rotterdam-based, popular with academia and the energy sector.
- **Beelen Relocation** — independent and personal, used a lot by mid-sized employers in Utrecht and Eindhoven.

A standard service package includes most of the following:

**Immigration and visa support.** They prepare your IND application, MVV entry visa if you need one, and the residence permit appointment after arrival. They know which documents the Dutch [Immigration and Naturalisation Service (IND)](https://ind.nl/en) actually accepts and which apostille variants will get rejected at the counter. For complex cases — non-EU partner, dependent children, knowledge migrant transitions — this alone can save weeks.

**Home search.** They scout, shortlist, and tour rental properties on your behalf, often before the listings go public. They handle the contract negotiation, deposit logistics, and the *huurovereenkomst* itself. This is the line item most people get value from in a tight market.

**BSN and municipal registration.** Booking your BSN appointment, sitting with you at the *gemeente*, and making sure your registration goes through cleanly the first time. The [official government guide](https://www.government.nl/topics/immigration-to-the-netherlands) is clear in theory; in practice the queues and document checks catch a lot of people out.

**School search.** Identifying international schools, bilingual *tweetalig onderwijs* options, and Dutch primary schools that take non-Dutch-speaking children. They handle the waiting list applications and trial-day logistics. For families, this is often the single biggest justification for hiring a relocation company at all.

**Settling-in services.** A half day or full day on the ground walking you through your neighbourhood — supermarkets, GP registration, the DigiD office, public transport OV-chipkaart, even where to dump your moving boxes. It feels luxurious until you arrive jet-lagged with two kids and a suitcase.

**Departure services.** If your contract ends and you leave the country, the same company can handle deregistration, utility cancellations, deposit return, and end-of-tenancy cleaning.

Smaller boutiques will pick a subset of these and price accordingly. Full-service global firms package them as tiered bundles.

### What is usually *not* included

Just as important as what relocation companies do is what they do not do. The line items I see clients assume are covered, that almost never are:

- **Furniture and appliance purchasing.** They will not buy you a fridge or a sofa. Some offer paid "settling assistance" days where a consultant joins you at IKEA Delft or Coolblue, but it is billed separately.
- **Pet relocation.** A few partner with specialist pet movers but the cost (€600–€2,200 per animal depending on origin) sits outside the package.
- **Driving licence exchange.** They can explain the 185-day rule and the RDW application, but the actual paperwork is yours.
- **Tax filing.** They will hand you off to a partner tax adviser. Your first Dutch *aangifte* is on you.
- **Translation of personal documents.** Sworn translation by a *beëdigd vertaler* runs €40–€120 per document and is usually billed at cost on top of the package.
- **Vehicle import.** BPM exemption paperwork for the car you ship in is a separate specialist service.

I have seen clients add €2,000 in line-item extras to a quoted €5,500 package because none of these were in the original scope. Read the inclusions list line by line before you sign.

## Cost Comparison: Full-Service vs DIY

Here is what I actually see in client quotes and personal experience for a 2026 move.

### Full-service relocation package

| Tier | What is included | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Essentials (single person) | Visa support, BSN, home search (2–3 viewings), arrival day | €3,500 – €5,500 |
| Family standard | Above plus school search, partner orientation, settling-in week | €6,000 – €8,000 |
| Premium / executive | Above plus area tours, full school placement, six-month aftercare | €8,500 – €12,000+ |

These are 2026 list prices for the Randstad. Outside Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht, expect 10–20% lower. Corporate clients with multi-hire contracts negotiate discounts of 15–30%.

### DIY relocation costs

| Item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| IND application fee (highly skilled migrant) | €380 |
| Document translations and apostilles | €150 – €400 |

Cash outlay for a DIY move where your employer covers the visa fee usually lands at €500–€1,500. Add €1,200–€3,000 if you need short-term housing while you search, which is the most common hidden cost people forget.

## When a Relocation Company Is Worth Every Cent

After comparing dozens of quotes with clients, four situations consistently justify the full package.

**1. Your employer is paying for it.** This is the obvious one but worth stating: if a relocation package is part of your offer and reimbursed tax-free as extraterritorial costs, take it. Do not be polite and decline. The cost is not coming out of your salary, and the time savings are real.

**2. You are moving with a family.** Two adults plus children multiply every administrative step. School waiting lists in Amsterdam-Zuid and The Hague's international districts open and close in cycles you cannot predict from abroad. School search alone — done well — is worth €1,500–€2,500 of the package. Add partner career support and you justify another €1,500. See [Dutch schools for expat children in 2026](/guides/daily-life/international-schools-netherlands-2026/) for what the school search actually involves.

**3. You have a tight start date.** If your employer needs you on the ground in six weeks and you are still abroad with no Dutch network, the home-search bottleneck will eat the timeline. A relocation company has agent relationships that compress a four-month housing search into two to four weeks. That alone can save you from a corporate housing bill of €3,000–€5,000 per month.

**4. Your visa case is complex.** Non-EU spouse, dependent children, prior immigration history, partner with a different employer, or a switch from one residence permit type to another. Anything that does not fit the standard knowledge migrant template benefits from someone who files these for a living. The €1,000–€1,500 visa support line pays for itself the first time a document avoids rejection.

## When DIY Wins

I did my own move and I would do it again in three of the following four scenarios.

**1. Solo move, EU/EEA passport.** No IND application, no MVV, no visa fee. Walk into a *gemeente*, register, walk out with a BSN appointment, and you are 60% of the way there. Spending €3,500 to manage what is essentially a one-day administrative task is overpaying.

**2. Flexible timeline (3+ months).** If you can stay with a friend, sublet, or use a HousingAnywhere mid-term rental for the first two months, you can run the housing search at your own pace. The Dutch [housing market for expats](/guides/housing/finding-housing-netherlands-expats-2026/) rewards patience and physical presence more than budget.

**3. Basic Dutch literacy or strong English.** Every *gemeente*, IND office, and *Belastingdienst* desk staff member in major cities now operates in English. The bureaucracy is far more accessible than its reputation suggests, especially if you can read signage and forms with the help of a translation app.

**4. You enjoy logistics.** Some people find planning a move energising. If you are one of them, a relocation package will feel like an expensive way to take the interesting bits away from you.

The fourth scenario where DIY does *not* win: tight timeline with no Dutch network and a corporate-grade housing budget that you have to find yourself. That was me. It hurt.

### The hidden DIY cost almost nobody calculates

When I tally up my own DIY move honestly — not the cash receipts, but everything — the picture is less rosy than the €1,200 I officially spent.

- Four months of temporary accommodation in a friend's spare room, which I covered with home-cooked meals, weekend childcare for her son, and one shared holiday. Roughly €1,500 in real economic value.
- Twelve viewings averaging three hours each including travel from Haarlem to Amsterdam Oost. Thirty-six hours of evenings and weekends.
- Two rejected applications that involved gathering payslips, employer letters, and bank statements I had to chase from abroad. Another ten hours.
- A €600 short-notice flight back to my home country to sign a notary document I had not realised I needed, because no one walked me through the checklist.

Add those up and the "free" DIY move cost me about €2,400 in real money and roughly 80 hours of life I would gladly have paid €1,500 to avoid. That is the calculation the brochures never show you. Whether it is worth it depends entirely on what you would have done with those 80 hours and €2,400 instead.

## How to Vet a Relocation Company

The relocation industry is unregulated in the Netherlands. Anyone can call themselves a relocation consultant. Here is the checklist I run before recommending one to a client.

**1. EuRA membership and the EGQS+ seal.** The [European Relocation Association](https://www.eura-relocation.com) maintains a public directory of members and a separate list of audited members holding the EuRA Global Quality Seal Plus. EGQS+ requires a third-party audit every three years. EMG, Crown, and Erasmus all carry it; many smaller players do not, and that is fine — but the absence should prompt more reference checks.

**2. Written, itemised quote.** A serious provider sends a one-page quote with every service line priced individually. If they refuse to break down the package and only quote a single number, walk away. You cannot negotiate or compare what you cannot see.

**3. Three references from clients in the last twelve months.** Ask specifically for references with similar profiles (single vs family, EU vs non-EU, your destination city). Call two of them. Ask one question: "What went wrong, and how did they handle it?" The way a company recovers from problems matters more than the absence of problems in marketing copy.

**4. Contract clarity on scope and overruns.** Read what counts as one "viewing." Is a video tour a viewing? If a property falls through after contract submission, does the next viewing cost extra? What is the policy if your visa is delayed and the package extends past the agreed window? These edges are where you get billed for things you did not expect.

**5. Local consultant assigned to your case.** Avoid setups where you are routed through a global account manager who has never been to your destination city. Insist on a named consultant based in your destination who has done at least twenty placements there in the past year.

**6. Independent dispute resolution.** EuRA members commit to a complaints process. Non-members should still tell you in writing what happens if you are unhappy with the service. "We will work it out" is not an answer; a written escalation path is.

## My Take After Three Years of Coaching Expat Moves

If you have a corporate package on the table, take it. If you are self-funding, the maths almost always points to a hybrid — pay for housing search only, DIY everything else, and put the saved €4,000 toward a bigger deposit or a better neighbourhood. If you are EU, solo, and patient, do the whole thing yourself and put the saved €5,000 toward something that compounds (an ETF account, your 30% ruling tax adviser, a real holiday after the move).

What you should not do is wing it without a plan. The single biggest failure mode I see is not "they hired the wrong company" — it is "they hired no one, made no plan, and ran out of timeline." Whether you go with EMG, with a boutique, or with [verhuisplanner.app](https://verhuisplanner.app) and your own grit, decide early and commit.

Good luck. The Netherlands rewards the people who arrive prepared, and punishes the ones who improvise. Be in the first group.
