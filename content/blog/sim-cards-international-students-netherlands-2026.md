International students hit the Dutch phone market in a specific order, and it trips almost all of them up the same way. You arrive in late August. You cannot get a monthly phone plan, because that needs a BSN. You cannot get a BSN quickly, because municipality appointments in student cities are backed up in the first weeks of the academic year. And in the meantime the university portal, your housing provider and your bank all want a Dutch mobile number.

The solution is unglamorous and takes twenty minutes: buy a prepaid SIM immediately, use it for as long as your paperwork takes, and switch to a cheap monthly plan afterwards while keeping the same number.

## Phase one: your first weeks, on prepaid

Prepaid exists precisely for the situation you are in. It requires none of the things you do not yet have.

**What you need:** money, and in practice your passport, since some providers and retailers ask for identification during activation under their own policies even though the Netherlands has no blanket legal registration requirement for prepaid SIMs.

**What you get:** a Dutch number, working the same day, with a top-up bundle you buy month by month and can abandon whenever you like.

**Where to buy:** supermarkets (Albert Heijn stocks starter packs in most larger stores), phone shops, electronics retailers, and online with delivery to your address in a day or two. Starter packs run from a couple of euros to around €10; bundles are roughly €5–€20 per month depending on data.

**Which brand:** for students it matters less than the internet suggests. All the prepaid brands run on one of the three national networks, all of them cover student cities properly, and you will be on it for a matter of weeks. If you call non-EU family on actual phone numbers, the migrant-focused brands — Lebara and Lycamobile are the two you will see everywhere — have the cheapest international add-ons and English-language support. If you only use WhatsApp, buy whatever is on the shelf.

The one thing to do properly is **register the number everywhere from the start** and then keep it. Your bank, your university, DUO if you become eligible, your GP, your landlord: the point of getting the number early is that everything is built on top of it.

## Phase two: the BSN and IBAN gate

Two documents unlock the cheaper half of the market.

**Your BSN**, issued when you register with the municipality. In student cities the September rush means appointment waits, so book the moment you have an address. Our [BSN application guide](/blog/expat-bsn-application-guide-2026/) walks through the process and the documents.

**A Dutch bank account**, for the direct debit every postpaid provider requires. Students have good options here, including accounts that open quickly for internationals — see our comparison of [Dutch banks for international students](/blog/best-dutch-banks-international-students-2026/).

Providers use the BSN to run a credit check before approving a subscription. It is a light check for a €10 sim-only plan and a serious one if you finance a handset, which is the next thing to understand.

## Sim-only versus a phone included

Dutch providers will happily sell you a subscription with a new phone bundled in. For students, sim-only is almost always the better decision, for two reasons.

**Cost transparency.** The phone element is a loan with the interest folded into the monthly price. Spread over 24 months it looks cheap; totalled up it usually is not.

**Your credit file.** Financing a handset in the Netherlands is a credit agreement, and above the applicable threshold it is registered with BKR, the national credit register. That registration is not a black mark in itself, but it sits in the same file that a mortgage lender or a landlord's screening will eventually look at. Starting your Dutch financial history with an unnecessary consumer credit line is a poor trade for a phone you did not need. Our guide on [building a credit history in the Netherlands](/blog/how-to-build-credit-history-netherlands-expats-2026/) explains how the register works.

Bring the phone you own. Buy sim-only. If your phone dies, buy a replacement outright or second-hand.

## Choosing a plan that fits a degree, not a discount

This is where students lose the most money in the Netherlands, and the mechanism is always the same: a shop offers a two-year plan at a heavily discounted rate for the first year, the student signs, and then leaves the country eleven months later still owing the remainder of the term.

Some plain arithmetic to keep in mind:

| Your programme | Sensible contract | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Exchange semester (4–6 months) | Prepaid only | No subscription term survives a stay this short |
| One-year master's | Monthly cancellable sim-only | A 24-month term outlives your residence permit |
| Two-year master's | 1-year term, or monthly | Leaves room for a thesis extension or an early departure |
| Three-year bachelor's | 2-year term with promotional pricing | Long enough that the discount is real value |

Monthly cancellable plans typically cost a few euros more per month than the same bundle on a two-year term. Across a one-year master's that premium is far smaller than an early-termination settlement.

A related trap: introductory pricing. Dutch telecom marketing leans on discounts covering the first 6 to 12 months of a 24-month contract, after which the standard rate applies. Budget Thuis, for instance, lists an unlimited plan at €20 per month standard with a substantially lower rate for the opening months of a two-year term, and smaller bundles from a few euros a month. Always find the post-promotion price, and confirm current terms on the provider's own page before ordering.

## What the budget brands actually are

There are three physical networks in the Netherlands — KPN, Odido and Vodafone — and perhaps a dozen consumer brands sitting on top of them.

- **KPN network:** KPN itself, Simyo, Budget Thuis Mobiel and others.
- **Odido network:** Odido, plus resellers including Simpel and Ben.
- **Vodafone network:** Vodafone and its own sub-brands.

A budget brand on the KPN network is using the same masts as a KPN subscriber. What you give up is not usually coverage but extras: priority during congestion on some plans, premium speed tiers, physical stores, and occasionally the polish of the app. For a student in Amsterdam, Utrecht, Groningen, Rotterdam, Leiden, Delft, Maastricht, Nijmegen, Enschede, Tilburg or Eindhoven, the coverage difference is not something you will notice.

Budget mid-size bundles — think 10 GB to 30 GB with unlimited calls — sit in a low single-digit to mid-teens euro range at standard prices depending on brand and term, and unlimited plans start around €20 at standard rates. ## How much data do you actually need?

Student accommodation almost always includes internet, campuses have eduroam, and libraries, cafés and even most Dutch trains have some form of Wi-Fi. The result is that student data usage is lower than people expect — commonly in the 5–20 GB range per month.

What pushes it up:

- **Intercity commuting**, particularly if you watch video rather than listen to music.
- **Bad accommodation Wi-Fi**, which is common in older shared houses and quietly turns your phone into the household hotspot.
- **Tethering a laptop** for coursework when the building's connection fails.

Check the last three months in your phone's settings before you buy. If you are consistently under 15 GB, unlimited is a purchase of reassurance rather than capacity — we go through that trade-off in detail in the [unlimited data plans comparison](/blog/unlimited-data-plans-netherlands-compared-2026/).

## Calling and messaging home

Three separate situations, three answers.

**Family who use WhatsApp, Signal or FaceTime.** Nothing to buy. Data calls cost only the data, and voice calls use very little of it.

**Family in the EU/EEA on ordinary phone numbers.** Your Dutch bundle's minutes apply when you call from the Netherlands to an EU number in most plans, but not all — check whether international calling within the EU is included or charged separately, because "unlimited calls" often means unlimited *domestic* calls.

**Family outside the EU on landlines or non-data phones.** This is where the migrant-focused brands earn their place. Lebara and Lycamobile both sell international minute add-ons covering large lists of destination countries at rates far below standard international pricing. If you call India, Turkey, Morocco, Nigeria, the Philippines or Brazil regularly on real phone numbers, an add-on of a few euros a month is the cheapest route by a wide margin.

## Campus Wi-Fi, eduroam and your data

Eduroam — the academic network you will use on campus across the Netherlands and most of Europe — is properly encrypted and does not need any extra protection. The networks worth thinking about are the other ones: café Wi-Fi, station Wi-Fi, the open network in a shared house, the hotel on a field trip.

## Before you graduate: the exit checklist

Leaving the Netherlands with an active phone contract is a common and avoidable mess.

- **Give notice properly.** Dutch subscriptions after the minimum term are typically cancellable with one month's notice, but the term itself must be served.
- **Move your two-factor authentication** off the Dutch number before you lose it — bank apps, DigiD, university accounts, government portals. Many of these are painful to recover from abroad.
- **Decide whether to keep the number.** If you plan to return, a cheap prepaid SIM kept alive with occasional top-ups preserves it. Check the provider's inactivity policy, as unused prepaid numbers do get recycled.
- **Cancel the direct debit only after the final invoice**, not before, or you will collect a debt collection letter in a language you were hoping to have finished with.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which SIM card should an international student buy on arrival in the Netherlands?

A prepaid SIM, in your first days. It needs no BSN, no Dutch bank account and no credit check, and gives you a +31 number immediately. Switch to a cheaper monthly plan once your BSN and Dutch IBAN are in place.

### Can I get a Dutch phone contract without a BSN?

Not a postpaid one — providers require a BSN for the credit check and a Dutch IBAN for direct debit. Prepaid is the workaround until your municipality registration is complete.

### Do Dutch providers offer student discounts?

Generally there is no widespread student tariff. The saving comes from choosing a budget brand reselling on one of the three networks instead of buying directly from KPN, Odido or Vodafone.

### Should I sign a two-year contract if my degree lasts one year?

No. Leaving the country does not end the contract, and you normally pay out the remaining months. Choose a monthly cancellable plan for a one-year programme.

### How much mobile data does a student in the Netherlands actually need?

Commonly 5–20 GB per month, given accommodation Wi-Fi and eduroam on campus. Heavy intercity commuting with video streaming pushes it higher.

### What is the cheapest way to call family abroad from the Netherlands?

Data calling apps for anyone who uses them. For landlines and non-EU destinations, international minute add-ons from providers such as Lebara or Lycamobile are far cheaper than standard per-minute rates.

### Can I keep my Dutch number when I switch providers or after I graduate?

Within the Netherlands, yes — porting is a legal right and takes a day or two. Keeping it after you leave requires maintaining an active Dutch subscription or prepaid SIM, and is worth planning for if your bank uses it for verification.

## The short version

Get a prepaid SIM in week one and stop worrying about optimising it. Get your BSN and a Dutch bank account as fast as the appointment queues allow. Then move to a budget sim-only plan on a term that ends no later than your degree does, port your number, and leave it alone for the rest of your studies.

The total difference between doing this well and doing it badly is perhaps €200–€400 over a two-year master's, plus the far larger cost of not having a working Dutch number during the weeks when every institution in the country is asking you for one.
