There is a specific moment that happens to almost every newcomer, usually within the first week. A full trolley at Albert Heijn, a queue behind you, and a card reader that will not take the Visa that has worked in twenty other countries. The cashier says something in Dutch, gestures at a sticker, and you begin unloading your groceries.

It feels like a malfunction. It is not. Your card is fine, your bank is fine, and the terminal is working exactly as its owner configured it. What you have run into is a payment culture that made different choices from the Anglo-Saxon one — and, if you understand those choices, one you can navigate in about ten minutes of setup.

This piece explains the economics behind the refusal, clears up the Maestro question that generates so much confusion, and sets out the payment configuration that simply never gets declined here.

> **Quick answer:** Dutch retailers are not rejecting *your* card; many of them reject *all* credit cards, because domestic debit costs them a few cents per transaction while a credit card costs a percentage of the basket. The Netherlands built its everyday payments on debit cards and its online payments on iDEAL, and credit cards were never the default. Get a Dutch debit card as your primary instrument, keep a credit card for deposits and pre-authorisations, and use a <a href="https://go.expatnetherlandshub.com/wise?ref=/blog/why-dutch-stores-refuse-foreign-credit-cards-2026/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow sponsored noopener">multi-currency account</a> for anything in another currency.

## The economics: why a shop would turn down money

Retailers do not refuse payment methods out of stubbornness. They refuse them because of what they cost.

A domestic Dutch debit payment — what everyone here calls *pinnen* — is priced to the merchant as a small fixed fee per transaction, historically a few eurocents. A credit card payment is priced as a percentage of the amount, made up of the interchange fee that goes to the card issuer plus the acquirer's margin and scheme fees. EU rules cap consumer credit interchange at 0.3% and consumer debit at 0.2%, but the total merchant service charge sits above the cap, and crucially it scales with the basket.

Now apply that to a supermarket. Groceries are a high-volume, low-margin business — net margins in food retail are commonly in the low single digits. On a €40 basket, a percentage-based fee can cost several times what the fixed debit fee costs. Multiply by millions of transactions a year and the choice stops looking like customer service and starts looking like the P&L.

That is why the pattern of acceptance is so consistent once you see it. Where baskets are large and margins are fat — hotels, electronics, fashion, restaurants in the centre — cards are welcome. Where baskets are small and margins are thin — the bakery, the market stall, the neighbourhood supermarket — they are not. Albert Heijn and several budget chains historically blocked credit cards for exactly this reason, and while acceptance has been widening, especially in tourist areas and station branches, it still varies from one branch to the next.

The second reason is simply that there was never demand. Dutch consumers overwhelmingly do not use credit cards for daily spending. In 2025, Dutch cardholders made **5.83 billion debit payments** at Dutch points of sale worth around **€150 billion**, according to the Dutch Payments Association — 95% of them contactless, and 47% of them made with a phone or watch rather than the physical card. A retailer that accepts debit and nothing else is serving the overwhelming majority of its customers perfectly well.

## The Maestro question

Ask a Dutch friend why your card does not work and there is a fair chance they will mention Maestro. Here is what that was about, and why the answer has changed.

For decades the standard Dutch bank card ran on **Maestro**, Mastercard's debit scheme, or on **V PAY**, Visa's equivalent. Both worked well in physical shops across Europe and badly everywhere else: they generally could not be used for online purchases, and outside Europe acceptance was thin. That is the historical root of the strange asymmetry expats notice — Dutch people could not easily pay online with their bank card, which is precisely why the Netherlands invented a bank-transfer method for e-commerce instead.

That era is ending. **Mastercard stopped issuing new Maestro cards from 1 July 2023**, and Visa did the same for V PAY. Cards issued before that date remain valid until their expiry, which means the last of them leave circulation by around 2027. Banks replace them automatically at renewal, with nothing required from the cardholder.

The replacements are **Debit Mastercard** and **Visa Debit**. The difference matters more than a scheme rename suggests: the new cards carry a 16-digit card number and a CVC code, so they work for online purchases, and they are accepted worldwide anywhere the corresponding credit card scheme is accepted. Your Dutch debit card is now a globally usable card, which removes one of the main historical reasons an expat here would have wanted a credit card at all.

What the migration does **not** change is merchant behaviour at the Dutch till. A Debit Mastercard is processed as a debit transaction with debit economics; a credit Mastercard is not. A shop can and often does accept the first and refuse the second, even though both carry the same logo. This is the single most common source of confusion in 2026: the logo on the terminal tells you the scheme, not whether your credit card will be taken.

## The online picture: iDEAL, and now Wero

The other half of Dutch payment culture is online, and it is not card-based either.

**iDEAL** is a bank-transfer method: you choose your bank, authenticate in your own banking app, and the money moves directly. There is no card involved, no card number to steal, and — from the merchant's side — no chargeback risk and low cost. It handled more than **1.5 billion transactions in 2025** and accounts for roughly **70% of Dutch e-commerce payments**.

The practical consequence for a newcomer is that plenty of Dutch webshops treat cards as an afterthought or omit them entirely. Municipal payments, parking apps, ticketing sites, local delivery services and small independents frequently offer iDEAL, direct debit and buy-now-pay-later before they offer Visa. To use iDEAL you need a Dutch bank account at a participating bank — which is a strong practical argument for opening one early, and for choosing a provider that participates. Nothing you need to do now, but do not be surprised when the button changes name.

## When your foreign card *will* work, and what it costs you

Your home-country credit card is not useless here. It is reliable for hotels, airlines, car hire, larger chains, restaurants in city centres, and most international online purchases. Contactless works, Apple Pay and Google Pay work, and the pre-authorisation function that rental desks need works.

What it costs is another matter, and it is worth being explicit because the charge is designed not to be visible.

**Your issuer's foreign transaction fee**, if it has one, applies to every euro you spend. **The exchange rate markup** applies on top, typically in the 1.75% to 2.5% band on bank-issued cards, folded into the converted amount rather than itemised. And if a terminal offers to charge you in your home currency — *dynamic currency conversion* — decline it. Always choose to be billed in euros. The convenience of seeing a familiar currency is paid for with an exchange rate set by the terminal's operator rather than by your card scheme, and it is consistently worse.

Add those together over a year of living here and a foreign card as your primary instrument is one of the more expensive mistakes an expat can make quietly. Two percent on €1,500 of monthly spending is €360 a year for the privilege of not having opened a local account.

## The setup that never gets declined

Four components. None of them are exotic and the whole thing takes a couple of weeks to assemble.

**1. A Dutch debit card as your primary instrument.** This is the non-negotiable one. A Debit Mastercard or Visa Debit from a Dutch bank is accepted everywhere in the country that takes cards at all, works online, works abroad, and costs you nothing per transaction. Everything else in this list is a supplement to it.

**2. A Dutch bank account with an NL IBAN.** Needed for iDEAL, for direct debits (rent, health insurance, energy, phone), and for employers and landlords whose systems reject foreign IBANs. Our [bank account comparison](/guides/finance/best-bank-account-expats-netherlands-2026/) and the [ING versus Rabobank piece](/blog/ing-vs-rabobank-expats-2026/) cover the options; [opening one without a BSN](/blog/how-to-open-dutch-bank-account-without-bsn-2026/) is possible with some providers if you are still waiting on registration.

**3. One credit card, for the situations that require one.** Car rental deposits, some hotel pre-authorisations, and chargeback protection on large purchases. Not for groceries. A Dutch-issued card is cheaper to run than a foreign one because euro transactions carry no markup — either through your own bank or directly from International Card Services, the specialist that issues and administers most Dutch credit cards.

## Reading the till before you queue

A few habits that save the trolley-unloading moment.

Look for the scheme logos on or beside the terminal, not just on the door. Terminals commonly display which schemes are accepted, and a shop with a **geen creditcards** sign means it. A sign saying **alleen pinnen** means debit only — no cash either. **Pinnen ja graag** is an invitation to pay by card rather than cash.

Remember that the logo tells you the scheme, not the card type. A Mastercard logo does not guarantee your credit Mastercard is accepted, because the shop may accept debit on that scheme only. If it matters — a large purchase, a full trolley — ask before you start: *"Kan ik met creditcard betalen?"* gets you a yes or no in two seconds.

And do not read a refusal as a judgement about you or your bank. Nothing has been declined. The shop simply never offered the option.
