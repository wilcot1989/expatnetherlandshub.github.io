Here is a small mystery that lands in a lot of expat inboxes. You applied for a credit card through ABN AMRO. A few weeks later, correspondence starts arriving from a company called ICS, on a website you have never visited, asking you to create an account somewhere that is not your bank.

Nothing has gone wrong. You have simply met the company that runs most of the Dutch credit card market from behind the curtain.

International Card Services is not a brand that markets itself heavily to newcomers, and it is barely mentioned in the English-language expat guides. But if you hold a Dutch credit card, it is quite likely the organisation that sets your limit, sends your statement, handles your chargeback and decides whether your application succeeded. This piece explains what it is, what it sells, and what that means for you.

> **Quick answer:** ICS (International Card Services) is a Diemen-based card specialist founded in 1988, part of the ABN AMRO group, with its own DNB banking licence. It is the largest credit card issuer in the Netherlands. It sells Visa and Mastercard products under its own name — from around €38.95 to €225 a year depending on tier — and administers card programmes for other brands including ABN AMRO. You can apply directly whatever bank you use, provided you have a Dutch address, a Dutch phone number, income above the card threshold and no negative BKR registration.

## What ICS actually is

International Card Services BV was founded in 1988 and operates from Diemen, just outside Amsterdam. It is an independent subsidiary within the ABN AMRO group and holds a banking licence from De Nederlandsche Bank, plus permissions from the AFM to offer credit, savings accounts and insurance intermediation.

Its business is credit cards, specifically: issuing them, promoting them, managing the accounts and processing the transactions on the Visa and Mastercard networks. It describes itself as the largest credit card provider in the Netherlands, which in a country where credit cards are a minority payment method is a smaller crown than it sounds — but it is the right crown.

Two things follow from that structure and they are the two things worth remembering.

**First, ICS is a real bank, not a processor.** It holds the credit relationship, not merely the technology. When your limit is set, ICS sets it. When your application is assessed against BKR, ICS is the member consulting the register. The card is its product even when someone else's logo is on the front.

**Second, ICS operates behind other brands.** It runs co-branded card programmes for a range of Dutch names, most visibly ABN AMRO's consumer credit cards. This is why the numbers, tariffs and terms for an ABN AMRO card frequently appear on icscards.nl rather than on abnamro.nl, and why card disputes go to ICS rather than to your branch.

## The 2026 card range

ICS structures its own-brand cards by fee and insurance cover rather than by rewards. There is no meaningful points programme to compare, for the structural reason that EU rules cap consumer credit interchange at 0.3% — there is not enough revenue in a European card transaction to fund an American-style rewards scheme.

On the Visa side, the range as listed in 2026:

| Visa World Card | €42.95 | 180 days | €1,000 | €2,000 |
| Visa World Card Panda | €42.95 | 180 days | €1,000 | €2,000 |

| Visa World Card Platinum | €175 | 365 days, longest cover | €2,000 | €3,000 |

A parallel Mastercard line runs across Classic, Gold and Black tiers with fees reported from roughly €38.95 up to €225 per year. All the cards carry contactless payment, Apple Pay and Google Pay support, 24/7 fraud monitoring and an optional deferred payment facility.

Fees change — the Gold increase scheduled for 15 September 2026 is itself the evidence — so treat the table as orientation and read the current figure on the application page.

## The ABN AMRO relationship

If you bank with ABN AMRO, this is the part that resolves your confusion.

ABN AMRO sells a credit card under its own name, on the Mastercard network, priced as a monthly contribution — reported at around €2.55 per month in 2026, with a Gold tier costing considerably more per year. It is settled by direct debit from your ABN AMRO current account and appears in the banking app.

ICS administers it. That means:

- **A separate portal and app** for card transactions, statements and settings, in addition to the summary shown in the ABN AMRO app.
- **Card customer service goes to ICS**, including disputes, chargebacks, limit changes, fraud reports and a lost or stolen card.
- **The card's terms sit in the ICS-administered programme**, which is why the interest rate on the spread payment facility — stated at 10% effective annually — is published on ICS service pages.

## Applying: what ICS checks

ICS publishes its conditions plainly, which is refreshing. To apply you need to be at least 18, have an address in the Netherlands and a Dutch (mobile) phone number, meet the income requirement for the specific card, and have no negative BKR registration.

The income figures reported for the mainstream cards cluster around €1,500 net per month, with some products quoted from roughly €1,150 and the premium tiers set higher. Because these are card-specific and revised over time, read the requirement on the page for the card you want.

Two aspects matter particularly for newcomers.

**An empty BKR file is not a barrier.** ICS consults the register for arrears codes and negative registrations, not for a history of successful borrowing. There is no Dutch credit score, so there is nothing you could have built in advance and no penalty for arriving without one. What blocks an application is a negative registration, not an empty one. The full explanation is in [how to build a credit history in the Netherlands](/blog/how-to-build-credit-history-netherlands-expats-2026/).

**Your spending limit is set from income and existing obligations.** ICS determines the limit rather than letting you name it, based on your declared income and any credit already registered against you. If the limit offered is lower than you hoped, that is usually a reflection of documented income rather than a judgement about you — and a modest limit is quietly the better outcome anyway, because a mortgage adviser will later subtract the full limit from your borrowing capacity regardless of your balance.

## BKR registration: the nuance

This trips people up, so it is worth being precise.

Consumer credit above €250 running longer than one month is reported to BKR. A credit card with a deferred or spread-payment facility above that threshold falls into the category and is registered. ICS's own guidance notes that simply using the card and settling the balance in full each month does not in itself create a reason for a registration.

The practical upshot: ask the issuer which category the specific product falls into, and set your direct debit to full monthly settlement unless you have a considered reason to borrow. A plain registration is neutral in the register's own terms, but an open limit is not neutral to a mortgage lender.

Note also that the register is in flux. The Wet stelsel kredietregistratie, adopted by the Tweede Kamer in February 2026, would give BKR a statutory basis and shorten the retention term, but it still requires Eerste Kamer approval before anything changes. We track this in the [BKR and phone contracts piece](/blog/dutch-phone-contracts-bkr-credit-registration-2026/).

## What ICS cards do not solve

An ICS card is a decent European credit card. It is not a solution to two problems expats often hope it will fix.

**It does not make you widely accepted at Dutch tills.** Credit card acceptance in Dutch retail is patchy by design. Supermarkets, market stalls, bakeries and small independents frequently take debit cards only, because domestic debit costs them a few cents per transaction while a credit card costs them a percentage of the basket. Your Dutch debit card stays your everyday instrument no matter which credit card you hold. We go through the economics in [why Dutch stores refuse foreign credit cards](/blog/why-dutch-stores-refuse-foreign-credit-cards-2026/).

**It does not give you cheap foreign currency.** Card issuers apply an exchange rate markup on non-euro transactions, typically in the 1.75% to 2.5% band, and it is folded into the converted amount rather than shown as a fee. For a population that spends across currencies routinely, that is the largest recurring cost of a bank-issued card and it dwarfs the annual fee.

The fix is to keep the two functions separate. ## Should you go direct to ICS or through your bank?

**Through your bank** if you hold an ABN AMRO, ING or Rabobank account and want the cheapest, most integrated option. The monthly contribution on a bank card is lower than the annual fee on a standalone ICS card, and settlement is automatic.

**Direct to ICS** if you bank with a provider that offers no credit card — which covers most of the fintechs expats start on — or if you specifically want the bundled insurance at the Gold or Platinum tier, or if your bank's card has a limit or a fee you do not like.

**Neither** if your honest reason for wanting a credit card is foreign spending or building a score. The first is better solved by a multi-currency account; the second is solving a problem that does not exist in this country.

## Practical things to do once you have the card

Set the direct debit to settle in full each month, so the interest rate never becomes relevant. Register on the ICS portal immediately rather than at the point of a dispute, because that is when you least want to be resetting a password. Note the ICS emergency number separately from your bank's, since a lost card is an ICS matter. Check what insurance the card actually includes before buying a travel policy that duplicates it. And keep the limit at a level you would be comfortable explaining to a mortgage adviser.
