Nobody moves to the Netherlands worrying about parking permits. Then you sign a lease in a nice central street, discover that the residents' permit for your area is either €600 a year or unavailable at any price, and realise that the cheap-looking apartment came with a car problem attached.

The Dutch permit system is not a single national scheme. Every municipality sets its own tariffs, its own zones, its own caps and its own visitor arrangements, and the differences between cities are far larger than most people expect — the same car, parked on the street, can cost a hundred euros a year in one city and well over a thousand in the central zone of another. This guide sets out what the four largest cities charge in 2026, how rationing and waiting lists actually work, and how to decide whether to keep a car at all.

All figures are the published 2026 amounts at the time of writing and change annually. Check your own city and your own address before budgeting.

Amsterdam prices per permit area, issues in six-month blocks that renew automatically, caps permits at zero, one or two per address, and runs waiting lists in areas where the cap is reached. Second permits cost multiples of first permits everywhere.

## How the Dutch Permit System Is Structured

Four features are common to nearly every city, and understanding them saves a lot of confusion at the counter.

**Permits are digital and tied to a licence plate.** There is no card in the windscreen. Your registration is in the municipal system, and enforcement happens by camera — the scan cars that drive slowly past parked cars reading plates. Change car and you must change the plate on the permit, or you are parked illegally in front of your own house.

**Eligibility runs on the population register plus vehicle ownership.** You must be registered at the address in the BRP, and cities generally require that you are the registered keeper of the vehicle. Amsterdam's regulation states the condition directly: the resident must be the *houder* of the motor vehicle. Lease and company cars typically need a separate declaration route.

**Supply is capped, not merely priced.** This is the part that surprises people arriving from cities where a permit is an administrative formality. Amsterdam's parking regulation allows the city to set the number of permits available per residential address at **zero, one or two**, by area. Zero is a real setting. Where the cap is reached, applications are processed in order of receipt and you join a waiting list.

**Second permits are priced punitively, on purpose.** In The Hague a second permit is roughly three and a half times the first. In Rotterdam it is about two and a half times. In Utrecht's zone A2 the second permit costs more per quarter than the first does — €242.76 against €97.11. Two-car households in Dutch cities are a policy target, not a customer segment.

## What It Costs in 2026: The Big Four

### The Hague

The cheapest of the four large cities, and by a clear margin.

| Item | 2026 amount |
|---|---|
| First resident permit | €99.20 per year |
| Second resident permit | €346.65 per year |
| Application fee | €17.70 |

The application fee is charged when you apply, so budget it as a one-off on top of the first year. The city publishes eligibility conditions per area; a permit is not automatic simply because you live in a paid-parking zone.

### Rotterdam

Priced monthly, which makes it easy to compare against a garage space.

| Item | 2026 amount |
|---|---|
| First resident permit | €9.50 per month (≈€114 per year) |
| Second resident permit | €25.70 per month (≈€308 per year) |
| Visitor allowance | 500 hours per year at €0.13 per 10 minutes |

The visitor allowance is generous compared with other cities and is the single best-value element of the Rotterdam scheme if you have people staying regularly.

### Utrecht

The most expensive of the four in its central zone, and the clearest example of why "what does a permit cost" has no single answer.

| Zone | First permit | Second and further permits | Visitor rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | €164.34 per quarter | — | €4.00 per hour |
| A2 | €97.11 per quarter | €242.76 per quarter | €3.47 per hour |
| BC | €42.27 per quarter | €105.72 per quarter | €2.67 per hour |

Visitor rates are set at half the short-stay tariff and capped at 70 hours per quarter in zones A1 and A2, and 120 hours per quarter in zone BC. A first permit in zone A1 works out at roughly €657 a year — about six and a half times what the same thing costs in The Hague.

### Amsterdam

Amsterdam does not publish one price, because the tariff is set per permit area. That is a deliberate design: the scarcer the street space, the higher the charge. The mechanics that matter:

- **Permits run for six months** and are renewed silently for another six as long as you still meet the conditions. So the number you see quoted is often per half-year, not per year — a frequent source of nasty surprises when people compare cities.
- **Per-address caps of zero, one or two** are set by area, so a household's entitlement is a property attribute as much as a personal one.
- **Waiting lists operate by date and time of application.** In some areas a permit is issued straight away; in others the wait is substantial. There is no way to jump the queue and no discretion at the counter.
- **Environmental conditions apply** to the vehicle, set out in the regulation's appendix.

Secondary sources commonly quote something in the range of €150 to €300 per six months for a first permit in Amsterdam, rising considerably in central areas — but the only figure worth planning on is the one the city shows for your own address. Amsterdam publishes both the tariff and the waiting-list situation per address through its own address checker on amsterdam.nl. Look it up **before** you sign the lease, not after.

### Smaller cities

Outside the big four, tariffs are generally lower and caps rarer, but the structure is the same: zones, per-address entitlement, digital permits, visitor schemes. Eindhoven, Groningen, Haarlem, Leiden and Delft all operate paid-parking zones with resident permits, and each publishes its own current tariff table in a municipal parking tax regulation (*verordening parkeerbelastingen*). Those regulations are public — searching for your city's name plus "verordening parkeerbelastingen 2026" finds the authoritative amounts rather than a summary.

## The Housing Decision Hiding Inside the Parking Question

For anyone arriving with a car, or intending to buy one, permit availability belongs in the house-hunting checklist alongside rent and commute. Three questions to ask about a specific address:

1. **Is a permit available at all at this address, right now?** Not "in this city" or "in this neighbourhood" — at this address, in this permit area, today.
2. **What is the wait if it is not?** Months and years are both real answers, and they lead to different decisions.
3. **What is the annual tariff here?** A €650 permit is roughly a €55-a-month standing cost, which changes the arithmetic of a car you use twice a week.

If the answer set is unattractive, the alternatives are not exotic. A rented garage space or a monthly subscription in a commercial car park is available immediately, and in the most expensive central zones is competitive with the permit — worth pricing rather than dismissing. Park-and-ride sites at the edge of the big cities are designed for exactly this, and the combination of a P+R space and public transport is what a large share of city residents actually do. And in the densest districts, the honest answer for many expats is that a car is a liability: the [cost-of-living comparison between Amsterdam and Rotterdam](/blog/cost-of-living-amsterdam-vs-rotterdam-2026/) shows how much of a city budget a car can absorb once parking is priced properly.

The permit is not the whole bill.

## Practical Steps When You Arrive

1. **Register at your address in the BRP first.** Nothing on the parking side works before that.
2. **Check the permit area, tariff and waiting list for your exact address** on the municipality's own site. Do this while house-hunting if you can.
3. **Apply the day your registration is processed.** Where lists exist they run on date and time of application, so a week's delay is a week further back.
4. **Register the right plate.** Update it whenever the car changes, including a replacement lease vehicle.
5. **Activate the visitor scheme.** It is cheap, it is per-household, and people routinely forget it exists until a friend gets a fine.
6. **Price the alternatives once, properly.** Garage rental, P+R plus transit, or no car — with the permit tariff and the urban insurance premium included, the comparison sometimes goes the other way.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a resident parking permit cost in the Netherlands in 2026?

It depends on the city and the zone. The Hague: €99.20 a year for a first permit, €346.65 for a second, plus a €17.70 application fee. Rotterdam: €9.50 a month, €25.70 for a second. Utrecht: €164.34 per quarter in zone A1, €97.11 in A2, €42.27 in BC. Amsterdam prices per permit area and publishes the amount per address.

### Is there a waiting list in Amsterdam?

In many areas, yes. The city can cap permits at zero, one or two per residential address, and applications are handled in order of receipt. Waiting times differ per permit area, so check your specific address on amsterdam.nl.

### Can I get a permit for a lease or company car?

Usually, but through a different route than a privately owned car, because cities require the applicant to be the registered keeper. Ask the municipality what declaration they need from the lease company or employer.

### Why is a second permit so much more expensive?

Because it is meant to be. Discouraging second cars in dense areas is an explicit aim of Dutch urban parking policy, and the tariff structure reflects it — a second permit commonly costs two to three times the first, and in some Utrecht zones more than that.

### Is a visitor permit worth it?

For most households, it is the best-value part of the scheme. Rotterdam gives 500 hours a year at €0.13 per 10 minutes; Utrecht sets visitor rates at half the short-stay tariff with a quarterly cap. Activate it when you get your own permit.

### What if no permit is available at my address?

Then your options are the waiting list, a rented private space, a commercial car park subscription, a P+R arrangement, or no car. All four are used routinely, and the economics in central zones are closer than people expect.

### Does city parking change what car insurance I should buy?

It changes the price and it strengthens the case for casco cover, because a car parked on the street overnight carries more theft and damage risk. Compare across insurers rather than assuming — location is weighted differently by different companies.
