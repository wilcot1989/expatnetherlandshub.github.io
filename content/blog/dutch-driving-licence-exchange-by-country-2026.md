Almost every guide to the Dutch driving licence explains the process: go to the gemeente, hand in your licence, wait for the RDW, collect the new one. That is the easy half. The half that decides whether you spend €55 and six weeks or several thousand euros and a winter of lessons is a single prior question: **are you allowed to exchange at all?**

Eligibility runs on country groups, not on how good a driver you are or how long you have held the licence. This guide sorts the routes in the order the RDW applies them, so you can find your own case in a couple of minutes and stop reading forum threads written by people in a different category. Rules and lists are amended periodically, so verify your own position with the RDW before you act on anything here.

> **Quick answer:** There are four possible outcomes. An EU or EFTA licence can always be exchanged. A licence from a country on the RDW's designated list can be exchanged, subject to a 185-day residence condition in the year you obtained it, and for several of those countries only for category B. Anyone holding the 30% ruling can exchange a licence from any country. Everyone else takes the CBR theory and practical exams. You also need to be registered in the BRP and to hold a qualifying nationality or residence status. Non-EU/EEA licences stop being valid for driving here 185 days after your BRP registration.

## Start With the Two Conditions That Apply to Everyone

Before the country question, two gates apply regardless of route.

**You must be registered in the Personal Records Database (BRP)** at a Dutch address. The exchange is handled by your municipality, which forwards the file to the RDW, and the municipality can only act for its own registered residents. If you have not registered yet, that is step zero — see [registering with the gemeente and getting your BSN](/guides/legal/bsn-registration-guide-2026/).

**You must hold a qualifying status.** The RDW lists this as: EU or EFTA nationality, or a temporary or permanent Dutch residence permit, or Turkish citizenship under Association Decision 1/80, or protection under the Temporary Protection Directive. A tourist or a person here on a short-stay basis cannot exchange, whatever licence they hold.

Both gates are about paperwork rather than driving, and both are checkable in an afternoon. Do that first.

## Route 1: An EU or EFTA Licence

This is the simplest case and the largest group of arrivals. A licence issued in an EU member state or in an EFTA country (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland) can be exchanged for a Dutch one. The RDW is explicit that such a licence **is allowed to be expired** when you apply — an unusual concession that catches people out in the other direction, because they assume an expired licence is worthless and start booking lessons.

Two practical notes:

- You are not obliged to exchange immediately. An EU/EEA licence remains valid for driving in the Netherlands for a long period, calculated from the licence's issue date rather than from your arrival. The RDW publishes the current rule; check your own issue date against it.
- There are still reasons to exchange voluntarily. A Dutch licence is accepted as ID here, is straightforward to replace if lost, and removes the friction some insurers and lease companies create around foreign documents.

## Route 2: The RDW List of Designated Countries

Outside the EU and EFTA, exchange is possible only for licences from countries and territories the Netherlands has designated. The list as published by the RDW covers, among others:

| Country or territory | Notes |
|---|---|
| Great Britain, Northern Ireland, Guernsey, Jersey, Isle of Man | Post-Brexit arrangement; on the list |
| Canada — Alberta and Québec only | Other provinces are not covered |
| Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan (Chinese Taipei), Israel | Category B (car) only |
| Monaco, Andorra, Gibraltar | All categories |
| Aruba, Curaçao, Sint Maarten and the former Netherlands Antilles territories | All categories |

Two conditions attach to this route and both matter:

1. **The 185-day condition.** You must have lived in the issuing country for at least 185 days in the year you obtained the licence. This exists to stop licence tourism, and it is the reason some people with a technically qualifying licence are refused: they took a short course abroad while living somewhere else.
2. **Category limits.** For several designated countries the exchange covers only category B. If you hold motorcycle or truck entitlements on such a licence, expect to re-qualify for those categories here even though your car entitlement transfers.

The RDW's own published list is the authoritative version and is amended from time to time. Do not rely on a list in a blog post — including this one — as your final answer. Look up your country on rdw.nl before you decide anything.

### If your country is not on the list

Then it is not on the list. There is no negotiation, no "but my licence is from a country with high standards" argument, and no discretionary exception at the counter. This is where large expat populations end up: the United States, India, China, Brazil, Turkey, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and most of the rest of the world are not covered by the designated-country route. Skip to Route 3.

## Route 3: The 30% Ruling Route

This is the widest route in the system, and the one most people discover too late. If you hold the 30% ruling, you may exchange a valid licence from **any** country, without a Dutch driving test. The RDW applies it to the ruling holder and, within its published terms, to accompanying family members.

Why it matters so much: an American, Indian, Brazilian or Australian licence holder who would otherwise face the full CBR route can, on the strength of the ruling, exchange for the municipal fee. The gap between those two paths is measured in thousands of euros and several months.

Practical points:

- **Check the timing.** The ruling has a limited duration, and the exchange route depends on your position at the time of application. If your ruling is approaching its end, do not leave the licence for later.
- **Bring proof of the ruling.** The confirmation from the Belastingdienst is the document that establishes this route; the municipality cannot verify it for you.
- **Partners should look at this too.** If your spouse holds the ruling and you hold a non-listed licence, ask specifically whether you fall inside the family provision rather than assuming you do not.

## Route 3b: You Previously Held a Dutch Licence

A smaller but real category. If you have previously held a valid Dutch driving licence (issued after 30 June 1985) and later swapped it for a foreign one — a common pattern for people who left the Netherlands and came back — exchange can be possible even from a country that is not on the designated list. If this is your history, mention it explicitly at the municipality, because nothing in the standard process surfaces it.

## Route 4: The CBR Exam Route

If none of the above applies, a Dutch licence means passing the CBR **theory** exam and the **practical** exam. There is no partial credit for twenty years of driving abroad.

What that involves, realistically:

- **Theory** is a separate exam on Dutch traffic rules, hazard perception and vehicle knowledge. It can be sat in English, which removes the language barrier but not the content barrier — Dutch priority rules, cycle-path rules and road-marking conventions genuinely differ from what you learned elsewhere.
- **Practical** requires you to drive to Dutch examination standards, which is where experienced drivers are most often surprised. Habits that pass unremarked at home (rolling stops, wide positioning, checking mirrors too little for the examiner's taste) fail here.
- **Cost** is dominated not by the CBR exam fees, which the CBR publishes, but by driving school lessons. Even experienced drivers typically buy a package of lessons to learn the examination conventions. Ask two or three local schools for an "experienced driver" quote rather than the standard beginner package, and get the CBR's current fees from cbr.nl.

One tactical note: because your foreign licence stops being valid 185 days after BRP registration, and because lessons plus two exams rarely fit inside that window if you start late, the CBR route needs to begin in your first weeks here — not in month five.

## The Two Clocks You Are Racing

The single most expensive misunderstanding in this whole topic is the validity period.

- **Non-EU/EEA licence: 185 days from your BRP registration.** After that, your licence no longer covers you for driving in the Netherlands. It does not matter that the licence itself shows an expiry date years away. Day 186 is the problem.
- **EU/EEA licence: a much longer window, keyed to the issue date.** Comfortable, but not infinite, and the calculation is not the same as for non-EU licences.

Two consequences follow. First, if you are on the CBR route, the 185 days are a planning constraint, not a formality. Second, if you are on an exchange route, apply well before the deadline rather than at it, because the elapsed time is municipal appointment plus health declaration plus the RDW's decision period — the RDW states it decides within 15 working days, but those days start only when it has your complete file.

## What It Costs

Numbers here are indicative, and every one of them should be checked against the source before you budget on it.

- **Municipal exchange fee.** Set per gemeente. Figures around €54 are commonly quoted for 2026, and some municipalities charge more — Amsterdam is quoted above €60. Your municipality publishes its own current fee.
- **CBR health declaration (Gezondheidsverklaring).** Required for exchanges of licences issued outside the EU/EEA, and it must be *approved* before the exchange can complete. It carries its own fee, published by the CBR, and can take time if any medical follow-up is needed. This is the step that most often stretches a six-week process into a three-month one.
- **Passport photo** to Dutch specification.
- **CBR route, if that is your path.** Theory fee plus practical fee plus lessons, with lessons being much the largest component.

## Sort the Insurance Alongside the Licence

The licence and the insurance are separate systems, and treating them as one sequence is what keeps people legal.

Third-party liability cover (WA) is compulsory for any vehicle registered in the Netherlands, from the moment it is in your name — not from the moment you drive it. So if a car is being registered to you while your licence file is still with the RDW, the insurance obligation has already started. Meanwhile, the premium you are quoted depends heavily on something unrelated to your licence: how many **schadevrije jaren** (claim-free years) a Dutch insurer will credit from your foreign history. Insurers differ substantially on this, and the difference between "twelve years claim-free" and "treated as a new driver" is one of the largest single swings in a Dutch motor premium.

## Mistakes That Cost Real Money

**Assuming your licence is exchangeable because it is "a proper licence".** Eligibility is a list, not a judgement of quality. Look your country up.

**Missing the 30% ruling route.** People from non-listed countries book driving lessons without ever checking whether the ruling gets them an exchange. This is the single most expensive oversight in the topic.

**Starting the CBR route in month five.** The 185 days include the time you spend finding a school and waiting for exam slots.

**Forgetting the health declaration.** For non-EU exchanges it is a prerequisite, not a parallel task, and an unapproved declaration stops everything downstream.

**Ignoring category limits.** A designated-country exchange may cover only your car entitlement. If you ride a motorcycle, check that separately before you sell the bike or ship it here.

**Cancelling foreign insurance before requesting the claim-free statement.** The document is easy to get while you are a customer and awkward afterwards.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which countries can exchange a driving licence in the Netherlands?

EU and EFTA countries always; the RDW's designated list — which includes the UK and Crown Dependencies, Alberta and Québec, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan, Israel, Monaco, Andorra, Gibraltar and the former Netherlands Antilles territories — subject to conditions; and any country at all if you hold the 30% ruling. Everyone else takes the CBR exams.

### Can I exchange a US driving licence in the Netherlands?

Not on the licence alone, because US states are not on the RDW's designated list. The realistic routes are the 30% ruling or the CBR theory and practical exams. Confirm against the current RDW list, which is the only authoritative source.

### Does the 30% ruling really let me skip the Dutch driving test?

Yes — that is exactly what it does, for a valid licence from any country, and within the RDW's published terms it can extend to family members. Bring the Belastingdienst confirmation to the municipality.

### How long can I drive on my foreign licence here?

A non-EU/EEA licence is valid for 185 days after your BRP registration. An EU or EEA licence is valid for much longer, on a rule keyed to the licence's issue date. Check your own dates on rdw.nl.

### What does the exchange cost in 2026?

A municipal fee — around €54 is commonly quoted, with cities such as Amsterdam higher — plus the CBR health declaration fee for non-EU/EEA licences, plus a passport photo. Municipalities publish their own current amounts.

### How long does the whole thing take?

The RDW decides within 15 working days of receiving a complete file. Add the municipal appointment beforehand, and for non-EU exchanges the time to get a health declaration approved. Weeks, not days.

### Do I have to exchange if I have an EU licence?

No, not while it remains valid here. Many people exchange anyway for the convenience of a Dutch document, but there is no eligibility risk in waiting — unlike the non-EU case, where waiting past day 185 leaves you without a valid licence.

## Final Thoughts

This is one of the few areas of Dutch bureaucracy where the outcome is fully knowable in advance. Your licence either sits in one of the three exchange routes or it does not, and ten minutes on the RDW site settles it. What turns a knowable situation into an expensive one is sequence: people discover the 185-day limit in month six, discover the 30% ruling route after they have paid for lessons, or discover the health declaration after booking the municipal appointment.

So do it in this order. Confirm your route on rdw.nl. If you have the 30% ruling and a non-listed licence, use it now rather than later.
