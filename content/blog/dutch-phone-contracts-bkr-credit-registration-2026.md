An expat signs a two-year phone contract in their first month here, walks out with a flagship handset for nothing up front, and thinks no more about it. Three years later a mortgage adviser pulls their credit register and there it is: a consumer credit line, opened for a telephone, quietly reducing the amount they can borrow.

This surprises almost everyone who did not grow up in the Dutch system. In most countries a phone contract is a phone contract. Here it is, in the eyes of the law, a loan — and it is recorded as one. This article explains why, what actually gets registered, what it costs you, and the three clean ways to avoid the whole thing.

> **Quick answer:** A handset paid off in instalments is consumer credit under Dutch law. If the financed portion exceeds €250, the provider must run an affordability check and register the credit at BKR, where it stays for five years after repayment and counts against your mortgage capacity while it runs. Sim-only plus a phone you own outright avoids it entirely, and is usually cheaper over the contract life anyway.

## How a phone became a loan

For years Dutch operators advertised subscriptions with a "free" handset. The Hoge Raad, the Dutch Supreme Court, took the view that this framing did not survive contact with reality: if the customer receives a device now and pays for it through inflated monthly instalments later, that is a purchase on credit — koop op afbetaling — regardless of how the marketing describes it. The consequence was that such subscriptions fall under the consumer credit provisions of the Wet op het financieel toezicht.

That reclassification pulled telecom providers into financial supervision. The AFM, the Dutch financial markets authority, now supervises the handset-credit part of their business. Providers offering device instalments need the appropriate licence, must comply with conduct rules written for lenders, and must treat prospective customers the way a lender treats a loan applicant.

From 1 May 2017, the practical machinery was completed: telefoonkrediet above €250 is reported to BKR, the central credit register, on the same footing as any other consumer credit.

## What is registered, and what is not

The distinction is precise and worth getting right, because a lot of English-language forum advice garbles it.

**Registered:** the financed device portion, when it exceeds €250 and runs longer than one month. That is the credit.

**Not registered:** the service. Your calls, texts, data bundle and monthly subscription fee are a service contract, not a loan, and never appear at BKR no matter how expensive the plan is.

So a €60 per month contract split into €25 of service and €35 of handset instalments over 24 months has a device credit of €840 — comfortably over the threshold, and registered. A €60 per month unlimited sim-only plan with no handset has no credit at all, and nothing is registered.

Two further points that trip people up:

- **The threshold is on the financed amount, not the phone's price.** If you pay a substantial amount up front and finance only the remainder, what matters is the remainder. Keep it at or below €250 and there is no registration.
- **Manufacturer and retailer instalment plans are not a loophole in themselves.** Whether an Apple, Samsung or retailer payment plan creates a registration depends on whether it constitutes consumer credit above the threshold. Some interest-free arrangements are structured to fall outside it; others are not. Ask the seller directly whether the arrangement is registered at BKR, and treat a vague answer as a yes.

## The credit check before you sign

Because it is credit, the provider must assess whether you can afford it. In practice that means a *kredietwaardigheidstoets*: the provider collects information about your income and your housing costs, applies the standard affordability logic, and consults BKR to see what other credit you already carry. If the sums do not leave you enough to live on, the contract should be refused.

For a newly arrived expat, this is the stage where things go wrong in unglamorous ways. You may not yet have Dutch payslips. Your BSN may still be pending. Your income may be in a foreign currency or paid by a foreign entity. Providers are working from a Dutch template and a refusal at this point is common and impersonal — it is an affordability model saying no, not a judgement about you.

It is also entirely avoidable, because none of it applies to a sim-only plan. No credit, no check.

## What a registration actually costs you

Here is the part expats underestimate.

**It is not a stain.** A plain BKR entry is factual and neutral. It records that a credit agreement exists. Dutch adults have them routinely and mortgage advisers do not flinch at one.

**It does reduce your borrowing capacity.** Dutch mortgage affordability rules deduct a monthly burden for outstanding consumer credit before calculating what you can borrow. That deduction is applied while the credit runs. A modest handset credit will not sink a mortgage application, but it will shave something off the maximum, and it does so for a phone you could have bought outright.

**An arrears code is the real problem.** If you fall meaningfully behind — typically in the region of two to four months — the provider may register an arrears code, the A-code. That is what makes a mortgage adviser wince, and it can mean refusal by mainstream lenders while it stands. A missed phone instalment is a very small reason to acquire a very large obstacle.

**It stays for five years after repayment.** That is the current retention period. On 12 February 2026 the Tweede Kamer adopted the Wet stelsel kredietregistratie, which gives BKR a statutory basis, shortens retention to three years and obliges providers to inform you within 30 days when an arrears code is registered. It still needs Eerste Kamer approval. Until it enters into force, five years remains the rule — so if timing matters to your plans, verify the current position rather than assuming the change has landed.

Our broader guide on [building a credit history in the Netherlands as an expat](/blog/how-to-build-credit-history-netherlands-expats-2026/) sets this in context, including the single most counter-intuitive point for arrivals from the US or UK: in the Dutch system, opening credit does not build anything. There is no score. Credit only ever subtracts from what you can borrow later.

## The maths, without the marketing

Set the credit register aside for a moment and look at the money.

A bundled handset contract spreads a device costing several hundred to well over a thousand euros across 24 months alongside the service. Providers are not charities; the financing is priced in. Over a full contract life, a bundle is typically more expensive than buying the same phone outright and pairing it with a competitively priced sim-only plan — and the sim-only market in the Netherlands is fiercely competitive in a way the bundle market is not.

The gap comes from two directions. The bundle locks you into a single provider's service pricing for two years, during which the sim-only market keeps moving underneath you. And the handset financing itself is rarely as free as the word "free" suggests.

There are genuine exceptions. Aggressive promotions on specific handsets can occasionally beat the standalone maths. The way to know is to do the arithmetic rather than trust the framing: total the 24 monthly payments plus any up-front amount, and compare against the phone's retail price plus 24 months of the sim-only plan you would otherwise take. If the bundle wins, and you are comfortable with a BKR registration for two years, take it knowingly.

## Three clean ways to avoid device credit

**Buy the phone outright and take sim-only.** The default recommendation. No credit, no affordability check, no registration, no lock-in on the service side, and you can re-shop your plan every year. **Keep the financed portion at or below €250.** If you want to spread some of the cost, pay enough up front that the remainder stays under the threshold. No registration follows. Confirm the exact financed figure in writing before signing, not the monthly payment.

**Buy a cheaper or refurbished handset.** Obvious, and effective. A mid-range or refurbished device paid for outright sidesteps the entire question, and the Dutch refurbished market is well developed with statutory consumer warranty applying to professional sellers.

If you have already signed a bundled contract and want out, remember that portability and cancellation are separate from the debt. Under the Wet Van Dam, once a contract has been tacitly renewed you may cancel with at most one month's notice. Within the fixed term you generally owe the remaining balance, and ACM rules do not allow a provider to block a number transfer because device credit is outstanding — the provider may send a final invoice, but it must release the number. ## Check your own file

Whether or not you think you have a registration, look. Log in at mijnkredietregistratie.nl using iDIN, the identity service built into your Dutch bank's login. The digital overview of your own data is free. Certified paper statements carry a fee, and there is never a reason to pay a third party to request your file on your behalf — BKR itself warns about phishing messages doing exactly that.

Do it once a year, and always before a mortgage conversation. Stale entries are common: a settled handset credit still showing as open, a closed facility still occupying capacity. Correcting a factual error costs nothing and you can raise it with the provider first and then with BKR. What no one can do, whatever they advertise, is delete an accurate entry for a fee.

## The short version

The Dutch treatment of phone contracts is unusual but internally consistent. Credit is credit, whether it buys a car or a handset, so it gets checked before it is granted and recorded while it runs. Nothing about that is punitive; a plain registration is neutral and an arrears code is the only genuinely damaging outcome.

But there is no upside either. Financing a phone in the Netherlands buys you nothing you could not get by buying the phone and taking sim-only, and it costs you an affordability check now, a slice of borrowing capacity for two years, and an entry that lingers for five years after the last instalment. For a device you will replace before the registration expires, that is a poor trade.
