The SIM card question splits into two completely different problems that get answered as if they were one. A visitor staying eleven days needs cheap data and nothing else. Someone who has just signed a rental contract needs a Dutch phone number, because half of Dutch daily life — bank verification codes, a landlord confirming a viewing, a Marktplaats seller with a second-hand bike — routes through a +31 number.

Buy the wrong one and the cost is small but annoying: an overpriced airport bundle you barely use, or three weeks of not being able to complete a bank onboarding because the SMS never arrives.

## If you are visiting from the EU or EEA: you probably need nothing

The EU roaming regulation — the "roam like at home" rules — lets you use your domestic calls, texts and data allowance in other EU and EEA countries without a surcharge. The Netherlands is covered, and the current regulation runs to 2032, so this is not something that is about to expire mid-trip.

Two caveats are worth knowing before you assume you are fine.

**Fair-use limits exist.** The rules are written for periodic travel, not for living abroad on a home-country plan. Providers may apply a fair-use policy to data, and many unlimited plans specify a roaming data cap that is well below "unlimited" — a Dutch unlimited plan, for example, may allow around 31 GB in the EU. Your home provider's policy works the same way in reverse.

**The UK is not in the scheme.** Since Brexit, UK plans are outside the EU roaming regulation. Some UK providers still include EU roaming voluntarily, others charge a daily fee, and a few charge per megabyte. Check your specific tariff rather than the general rule — this is the single most common expensive surprise for visitors to the Netherlands from Britain.

## If you are visiting from outside the EU: eSIM or prepaid

For a trip of a few days to a few weeks, the practical choice is between a travel eSIM bought before departure and a Dutch prepaid SIM bought on arrival.

**Travel eSIMs** (the well-known international brands operate in the Netherlands, though none of them is a partner of ours and we earn nothing on them) are data-only in most cases. You install a QR-code profile before you fly and the plan activates when you connect to a Dutch network. You keep your home number on your physical SIM for banking codes, and your data runs over the eSIM. There is no shop, no queue, no activation form in Dutch.

**Dutch prepaid SIMs** cost less per gigabyte for anything beyond a few days, and they give you an actual Dutch number. If your trip involves any Dutch admin — a viewing, a delivery, a bank appointment — the number is worth more than the data saving.

### Buying at Schiphol

Schiphol's arrivals halls have operator stores and independent phone kiosks selling prepaid tourist packages. Reported prices sit broadly in the **€10–€35 range** depending on data volume, with a typical operator tourist bundle around 2 GB at the bottom of that range and roughly 10 GB near the top, usually valid for 30 days. Airport retail carries a small premium over the same SIM in a city supermarket, and shop opening hours do not cover every night arrival.

Prices and bundle sizes change frequently and vary per outlet — check the current offer at the counter rather than relying on any figure you read online, including ours.

If you would rather not gamble on a kiosk being open, activate a travel eSIM before you fly and buy a local SIM later in the week if you decide you need a number after all.

## If you are moving here: prepaid first, always

New arrivals hit the same wall in the same order. You want a Dutch bank account, the bank wants a Dutch mobile number for verification. You want a monthly phone contract, the provider wants a BSN and a Dutch IBAN. Neither can go first.

Prepaid breaks the deadlock. Dutch prepaid SIMs are sold in supermarkets and phone shops, need no BSN, no credit check and no Dutch bank account, and give you a working +31 number the same day. Starter packs are typically a few euros to around €10, and monthly top-up bundles run roughly €5–€20 depending on data.

**On ID and registration:** the Netherlands does not have a blanket legal requirement to register prepaid SIMs against identity documents, unlike Germany, Spain or Belgium. In practice, individual providers and retailers apply their own checks — some online activation flows ask you to upload a passport, some shops ask to see one. Carry your passport when you go to buy one and the question never becomes a problem.

Realistically, the SIM should be day one or two: it unblocks nearly everything else.

## When to leave prepaid behind

Prepaid is the right entry point and the wrong long-term answer. The economics are simple: a prepaid bundle around €10–€15 typically buys you a handful of gigabytes, while a monthly sim-only plan in the same price band buys tens of gigabytes, and unlimited plans from budget providers start around **€20 per month** at standard rates — often lower during introductory promotions.

The switch requires two things you will not have on arrival:

- **A BSN**, because postpaid providers run a credit check that is keyed to it. Our [BSN application guide](/blog/expat-bsn-application-guide-2026/) covers the appointment and timing.
- **A Dutch IBAN** for the monthly direct debit (automatische incasso). See the [step-by-step Dutch bank account guide](/blog/getting-a-dutch-bank-account-step-by-step-2026/).

Once both exist, moving from prepaid to sim-only takes about half an hour, and number portability (nummerbehoud) is a legal right in the Netherlands — you keep the number you have been handing out for the last three months.

## Networks, coverage and what actually differs

The Netherlands has three physical mobile networks — KPN, Odido (the former T-Mobile NL and Tele2) and Vodafone. Every other brand you see on a supermarket shelf is a reseller running on one of them.

For a visitor, the practical difference is close to nil. All three cover the Randstad, the intercity rail corridors and every city you are likely to visit. Coverage differences show up in rural Zeeland, Drenthe and Friesland, on some regional train lines, and deep inside older buildings — which matters if you are moving to a village, not if you are spending a long weekend in Amsterdam.

What does differ, and what you should check before buying:

- **Speed caps on "unlimited" plans.** Several unlimited tariffs are speed-limited rather than volume-limited. The cheapest unlimited plans can be capped at speeds well below the network maximum.
- **EU roaming allowance.** Unlimited domestically rarely means unlimited while roaming; a cap in the tens of gigabytes is normal.
- **Language of support.** English-language apps and support are standard at the larger brands and patchier at some budget resellers.

## Dual SIM: the setup most new arrivals should use

Almost every phone sold in the last few years supports one physical SIM plus one eSIM, and many support two eSIM profiles. That means the choice between your home number and a Dutch number is a false one.

The standard arrangement: home SIM stays in the phone, kept alive for two-factor codes from your home bank and calls from family; Dutch SIM or eSIM handles data and everything local. Set the Dutch line as the default for outgoing calls and mobile data, and turn data roaming off on the home line so you never accidentally burn through home-country charges.

If your phone is single-SIM only, the sequence is: keep your home SIM in for the first few days, buy a Dutch prepaid SIM, then move the home SIM out once you have transferred your important accounts' two-factor settings to the Dutch number or to an authenticator app.

Whether an eSIM or a plastic SIM makes more sense for your specific device and situation is a longer discussion — we cover it in the companion piece on [eSIM versus physical SIM in the Netherlands](/blog/esim-vs-physical-sim-netherlands-expats-2026/).

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

**Buying 10 GB for a five-day trip.** With hotel and café Wi-Fi, most visitors do not clear 2–3 GB in a week. Buy small, top up if needed.

**Assuming an airport SIM is a bad deal by definition.** It carries a premium, but the premium is a few euros. If it saves you an evening of hunting for a phone shop with your luggage, it is defensible.

**Signing a two-year contract in week one.** You cannot, in practice — no BSN means no postpaid — but people occasionally get one through a partner or employer and regret the term length when they move again. One-month cancellable plans exist and cost slightly more; the flexibility is usually worth it in year one.

**Forgetting the post-promotion price.** Dutch telecom marketing leans heavily on introductory rates for the first 6–12 months of a 24-month term. The number that matters is what you pay in month 13.

**Treating the number as disposable.** Once your bank, employer, gemeente, insurer and doctor all have your Dutch number, changing it is genuinely painful. Port it when you switch providers rather than accepting a new one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a Dutch SIM card as a tourist in the Netherlands?

Often not. If your plan comes from an EU or EEA country, roam-like-at-home rules mean your normal domestic allowance works here at no surcharge, subject to fair-use limits. A Dutch SIM or travel eSIM becomes worthwhile if you are coming from outside the EU/EEA — including the UK, which left the scheme after Brexit — or if you are staying long enough that roaming stops looking like periodic travel.

### Where can I buy a SIM card at Schiphol Airport?

Schiphol's arrivals halls have operator stores and independent phone kiosks selling prepaid tourist SIMs, with packages broadly in the €10–€35 range depending on data. Airport pricing runs slightly above city retail. Activating a travel eSIM before you fly avoids the queue entirely.

### Do I need a passport or a BSN to buy a prepaid SIM in the Netherlands?

No BSN is needed, and the Netherlands has no blanket legal prepaid registration requirement. Providers and retailers may still ask for ID under their own policies, and some online activations request a passport scan, so bring your passport.

### How much data does a two-week trip actually need?

Most visitors use roughly 2–5 GB across two weeks for maps, messaging and browsing, given hotel and café Wi-Fi. Streaming video on the move is what pushes you past that.

### Is a travel eSIM better than a Dutch prepaid SIM?

For data on a short trip, usually yes — it works before you land and needs no shop. For anything requiring a Dutch number, no: travel eSIMs are typically data-only, and Dutch SMS verification needs a +31 number.

### When should a new arrival stop using prepaid and switch to a monthly plan?

Once you have a BSN and a Dutch IBAN and expect to stay beyond a few months. Sim-only plans are far cheaper per gigabyte, with unlimited tiers starting around €20 per month at standard rates.

### Can I keep my home number and use a Dutch number at the same time?

Yes, with dual SIM — typically one physical SIM plus one eSIM. Most new arrivals run their home number for existing two-factor codes and the Dutch number for everything local.

## The short version

If you are here for a holiday and your plan is European, do nothing. If you are here for a holiday from further away, a travel eSIM before departure is the least friction for the least money, and a Schiphol kiosk is a perfectly reasonable fallback.

If you are moving here, the priority is not the cheapest gigabyte — it is having a +31 number in your hand on day one, because that number is the key that unlocks the bank account, which unlocks the phone contract, which unlocks the sensible pricing. Buy prepaid immediately, spend a few months finding out how much data you actually use, then switch to a sim-only plan and port the number across.
