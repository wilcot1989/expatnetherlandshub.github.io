Ask five colleagues in a Dutch office which mobile provider they use and you will get five different brand names, most of which turn out to be the same three networks wearing different jackets. That is the single most useful thing to understand about the Dutch mobile market: the brand you pay and the network you actually use are two separate decisions, and expats routinely overpay because they only make the first one.

This article compares the market by that logic. Which network you want, then which brand on that network fits your usage and your tolerance for Dutch-language customer service. If you are looking for a starter overview of sim types instead, our [sim-only plans guide](/blog/best-sim-only-plans-expats-netherlands-2026/) and the [prepaid SIM comparison](/blog/best-prepaid-sim-card-netherlands-2026/) cover the arrival phase; this one is about picking a monthly plan you will keep.

> **Quick answer:** There are three Dutch networks — KPN, Odido and VodafoneZiggo — and everything else is a reseller on one of them. Odido and Vodafone's own-brand plans buy you English-language support, family bundles and non-EU roaming passes. Budget brands on the same masts cost a fraction of that. If your usage is ordinary and you can handle a chat-based support channel, a KPN-network budget brand such as Budget Thuis is the rational default; pay for a premium brand only if you specifically need the service layer.

## Step one: the three networks

Everything sold in the Netherlands runs over one of three radio networks.

**KPN.** The former state operator, still the widest-reaching network, particularly outside the Randstad, on regional rail lines and inside older buildings. Independent measurement firms including Opensignal have repeatedly ranked KPN at or near the top of the Dutch market for network consistency and 5G availability. KPN also wholesales heavily, which is why so many cheap brands sit on it.

**Odido.** The result of merging T-Mobile Netherlands with Tele2 and rebranding in 2023. Strong urban performance and competitive 5G speeds in the big cities. The T-Mobile name has been fully retired in the Netherlands, which confuses arrivals from Germany, Poland or the United States who go looking for it.

**VodafoneZiggo.** Vodafone's mobile network combined with Ziggo's cable business. Good in cities, generally behind KPN in rural reach. Its main pull is the bundling story: mobile plus Ziggo broadband and television on one bill.

## Step two: what the premium brands actually sell you

The radio signal is not the product difference. These four things are.

**English-language customer service.** KPN, Odido and Vodafone all operate English-speaking support by phone. Most budget brands are Dutch-first, and several are chat-and-webform only with no telephone line at all. For an expat in month two, with a porting problem and no Dutch, that gap is worth real money. For an expat in year four with a stable plan, it is worth almost nothing.

**Family and household bundles.** Multi-line discounts and mobile-plus-broadband combinations are a premium-brand product. Budget brands rarely offer them. A household with three or four lines can close much of the price gap by bundling — which is exactly why the premium brands push it.

**Non-EU roaming.** EU roaming is included everywhere by law. Travel beyond the EU is where the brands diverge sharply: the premium operators sell day passes and world bundles, while budget brands often simply charge per-megabyte rates you do not want to discover after the fact. If you fly to the UK, the US or Turkey several times a year, check this before price.

**Handset financing and repair services.** Premium brands run shops, take trade-ins and finance handsets. That last one is a mixed blessing — see the section on credit below.

## Step three: the price picture in 2026

Dutch sim-only pricing moves constantly, and the headline numbers you see advertised are usually promotional rates that revert after six or twelve months. Treat the figures below as a snapshot of list and promotional pricing observed in August 2026, not as a quote.

The shape of the market is stable even when the numbers are not:

- **Unlimited data** is the clearest comparison point, because everyone sells it. In August 2026 comparison sites listed Odido's unlimited plan around €25 per month and KPN's own around €30, while Budget Thuis on the KPN network advertised unlimited from around €20 with promotional pricing well below that. That spread — roughly €5 to €10 per month for the same masts — is the premium you are paying for the service layer.
- **Mid-range bundles** of 15 to 30 GB are where the budget brands look strongest, with promotional entry prices from a few euros a month. The catch is the revert price after the promotional period, which is the number that actually determines your annual cost.
- **Small bundles** under 5 GB have become almost a rounding error in price. If you only need a Dutch number and light data, almost any budget brand will do it for single-digit euros.

Two practical consequences. First, always look up the price *after* the promotional term, and multiply the difference by the contract length. Second, the cheapest brand this month is frequently not the cheapest brand next month, which is why comparison tools earn their place here.

## Matching a plan to how you actually live here

### You work in an office and have home Wi-Fi

You need less data than you think. Maps, messaging, music on the commute and the occasional video call add up to somewhere in the 5 to 15 GB range for most people. Any budget brand covers this comfortably. Pick by host network: KPN if you travel outside the big cities, Odido or Vodafone if your life is entirely Randstad.

### You commute by train across provinces

Coverage stops being theoretical. Trains are metal boxes moving fast between cells, and the differences between networks show up exactly there. This is the strongest case for a KPN-network brand, premium or budget. Also budget more data than the office worker above — trains are where streaming happens.

### You tether a laptop or work from cafés

Go unlimited, and read the fair-use terms rather than the headline. Every "unlimited" plan in the Netherlands has a speed policy and a roaming cap; what varies is how high the ceiling sits and whether tethering is explicitly permitted. Premium unlimited plans are usually the safer choice for heavy tethering because the terms are more generous and support is reachable if you get throttled.

### You have just arrived and have no BSN

You cannot take a postpaid contract yet. Start prepaid — Lebara and Lyca activate on a passport and are sold in supermarkets and phone shops — and switch to a sim-only plan once your BSN and Dutch IBAN are in place. Our [BSN application guide](/blog/expat-bsn-application-guide-2026/) sets out that sequence, and the [first-year pillar](/blog/first-year-netherlands-expat-pillar-2026/) puts it in order with banking and health insurance.

### You are a household of three or more

Run the maths both ways. A premium family bundle with multi-line discounts and a broadband combination can genuinely beat four separate budget lines, particularly if you are also buying home internet. If you are not bundling broadband, four budget lines usually win. Our [broadband comparison](/blog/best-broadband-internet-providers-expats-netherlands-2026/) covers the internet half of that decision.

## The contract-length trap

Dutch sim-only plans come in three flavours, and the difference matters more than the monthly price.

**Monthly cancellable** (*maandelijks opzegbaar*) costs a few euros more per month and lets you leave whenever you like. This is the correct choice if your stay in the Netherlands is uncertain, if you are on a short assignment, or if you simply do not want to think about it.

**Twelve months** is the market standard and usually where the best promotional pricing sits.

**Twenty-four months** shaves a little more off the monthly rate and locks you in for two years. Worth it only if you are settled and the revert price is genuinely low.

Then the part that costs expats the most money: **nothing improves automatically when your term ends.** Providers keep charging the contract rate indefinitely. What the law gives you is an exit, not a discount. Under the Wet Van Dam, once a consumer contract has been tacitly renewed you may cancel at any moment with a notice period of no more than one month, regardless of what the terms and conditions claim. Put a calendar reminder for eleven months after signing, re-run a comparison, and either switch or negotiate. That single habit is worth more than choosing the perfect plan in the first place.

## The handset question, and why it is a credit question

Dutch carriers will happily sell you a phone on instalments alongside your plan. Legally this is not a bundle discount; it is consumer credit. Following Dutch case law and AFM supervision, a financed handset above €250 triggers a creditworthiness assessment and a registration at BKR, the national credit register in Tiel. That registration then sits against your borrowing capacity for as long as the credit runs — including when a mortgage lender assesses you.

The clean alternatives are to pay for the handset outright, or to keep the financed portion below the €250 threshold, and pair either with a sim-only plan. ## Roaming: what is free and what is not

All Dutch consumer plans include EU roaming under the "Roam Like at Home" rules, covering the EU plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway, and extended to Ukraine and Moldova from January 2026. Your domestic allowance travels with you.

Three limits catch people out:

- **Fair-use data caps.** On unlimited plans, roaming data is capped at a set volume per month. Above it, providers may apply surcharges.
- **Permanent roaming.** The rules are designed for travel. If, over a rolling four-month window, you spend more time abroad than at home *and* your roaming usage exceeds your domestic usage, your provider may contact you and eventually apply surcharges. A Dutch sim used full-time from another country is not a loophole.
- **The UK and Switzerland are outside the scheme.** Both are charged separately, and the rates vary enormously between brands.

## Choosing, in one paragraph

Decide the network first: KPN if you leave the big cities regularly, any of the three if you do not. Then decide whether you are buying the service layer. If English-language phone support, family bundles and structured non-EU roaming matter to you, take the premium own-brand on your chosen network and accept the €5 to €10 monthly premium. If they do not, take the cheapest well-reviewed brand on the same network, choose a monthly-cancellable or twelve-month term, and re-compare every year. Everything else — the marketing, the brand names, the "free" phone — is noise around those two decisions.
