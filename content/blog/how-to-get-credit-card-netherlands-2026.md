"How do I get a credit card in the Netherlands?" is one of those questions where the honest answer starts by correcting the question. Newcomers ask it expecting the American or British version of the process: apply, get scored, get approved or rejected, then spend responsibly for a year to earn a better card. That machinery does not exist here.

What exists instead is a short list of factual conditions. Are you 18? Do you live at a registered Dutch address? Do you have a Dutch bank account in your own name? Does your net income clear the issuer's published floor? Is there an arrears code against you in the national credit register? Answer those five correctly and the card usually arrives. Answer one of them badly and no amount of good financial behaviour elsewhere will rescue the application.

This guide walks the process end to end: what the issuers check, which route suits which situation, what to do while an application is pending, and the traps that catch expats specifically.

> **Quick answer:** There is no Dutch credit score, so there is nothing to build first. Register at your municipality, open a Dutch bank account, then apply either through that bank (simplest, because it can see your income) or directly with a card issuer. Expect a minimum net income requirement in the region of €1,150 to €1,500 per month, an ID and income check, and a decision within days. ## The empty BKR file: why it is not the problem you think

Almost every expat arrives believing that a blank credit record is a handicap. In an Anglo-Saxon system it genuinely is — a "thin file" is scored as a risk in itself. The Dutch system is built on a different premise.

Stichting BKR in Tiel maintains a central credit register that every licensed credit provider must consult and report to. It is a factual list of credit agreements and arrears, not a rating. There is no number, so there is nothing to optimise. A person with no entries is simply a person with no consumer credit, which is the ordinary condition of a solvent Dutch adult rather than an anomaly.

The consequence for you is counterintuitive: the register does not reward you for having credit. It can only work against you, through an arrears code. And an open credit limit actively reduces your borrowing capacity when you later apply for a mortgage, because affordability rules assume a monthly burden on the whole limit rather than on your actual balance. Opening a card "to build history" is, at best, neutral and, at worst, a small self-inflicted wound to your future house purchase.

We cover the mechanics in detail in [how to build a credit history in the Netherlands](/blog/how-to-build-credit-history-netherlands-expats-2026/). The short version: get the card if you have a use for it, not as an investment in a score that does not exist.

## What issuers actually check

Strip away the assumptions and a Dutch credit card application tests five things.

**Age and legal capacity.** Eighteen or over. Straightforward.

**A registered Dutch address.** Not a hotel, not a friend's sofa, not a mail-forwarding service. You need to be registered in the Basisregistratie Personen at the address you give. Issuers verify this, and an address mismatch is one of the most common quiet rejections for newcomers.

**A Dutch bank account in your own name.** The card is settled by direct debit from a Dutch IBAN. This is why the sequence matters: bank account first, card second. If you have not done that step, start with our [step-by-step guide to opening a Dutch bank account](/blog/getting-a-dutch-bank-account-step-by-step-2026/).

**Income above a threshold.** Mainstream Dutch issuers publish minimum net monthly income requirements that generally sit in the €1,150 to €1,500 range depending on the specific card, with premium products set higher. Because the exact figure varies by card and changes over time, read it on the issuer's own application page rather than trusting a comparison site. The threshold is a pass/fail filter; it does not translate into a better card if you exceed it comfortably.

**A clean BKR record.** No arrears code (an A-code), no special code recording a settlement or write-off. A plain, current registration is not disqualifying on its own, but it consumes capacity.

Notice what is absent: length of residence, nationality, whether you have ever held credit before, and any behavioural history. Notice also what is implicit: your income needs to be documented and verifiable. A generous but undocumented income is worth less to a Dutch issuer than a modest, evidenced one.

## Route one: through your own bank

The path of least resistance is to ask the bank that already holds your salary. It can see the money arriving, which removes most of the verification friction, and the application usually lives inside the banking app rather than as a separate contract.

ABN AMRO, ING and Rabobank all offer credit cards to their own account holders. They are not, by international standards, exciting products: modest limits, no meaningful rewards programme, a monthly or annual fee. What they are is easy, integrated, and settled automatically from the account you already use. For most expats that is exactly the right trade.

The bank sells the card; ICS runs the statements, the online portal and the customer service. ## Route two: directly with a card issuer

You do not have to hold an account with the issuer. International Card Services issues Visa and Mastercard products directly to anyone who meets its conditions, regardless of which Dutch bank holds your current account. Its published requirements are the standard set: at least 18, a Dutch address, a Dutch phone number, income above the card-specific threshold, and no negative BKR registration.

The ICS range is tiered by annual fee and insurance cover rather than by rewards. At the time of writing the Visa World Card is listed at €42.95 per year, the Visa World Card Gold at €57.95 rising to €59.50 from 15 September 2026, and the Visa World Card Platinum at €175 per year, with a parallel Mastercard line running from roughly €38.95 to €225 per year. Fees change, so treat those as orientation and confirm on the application page.

## The application itself, step by step

**1. Get the prerequisites in place.** BRP registration, BSN, Dutch bank account, at least one payslip or a documented income stream. Trying to shortcut any of these produces a rejection that then sits in your own memory as evidence you are "not creditworthy", which is the wrong lesson.

**2. Pick one card and one issuer.** Applying to three at once does not increase your odds. Each application is assessed on the same facts, so a rejection from one is likely to be repeated by the others, and you have simply generated more paperwork.

**3. Fill in income honestly and in the format asked.** Note whether the form asks for net or gross, monthly or annual. Mixing these up is a routine cause of an application being kicked out, and it looks like misstatement rather than confusion.

**4. Choose the payment mode deliberately.** Most Dutch cards let you settle in full each month or spread the balance. Spreading is consumer credit: it accrues interest, and it is the version that gets reported to BKR. ABN AMRO's spread-payment facility, administered through ICS, is stated at 10% effective annually — cheap by international credit card standards, but still a cost you have chosen. Set the direct debit to full settlement unless you have a specific reason not to.

**5. Wait, then check.** Decisions typically arrive within days rather than weeks, with the physical card following separately. If you are refused, ask for the reason. Issuers will tell you, and the answer is almost always a single correctable fact.

## Mistakes expats make

**Applying before registering.** The address check is automated and unforgiving. Register first.

**Assuming a foreign card is a substitute.** It works online and at hotels, but it will fail you at supermarket tills and it carries a foreign transaction fee on every purchase. If you want to know why Dutch shops behave this way, we wrote a whole piece on it: [why Dutch stores refuse foreign credit cards](/blog/why-dutch-stores-refuse-foreign-credit-cards-2026/).

**Taking a high limit because it is offered.** The limit, not the balance, is what a mortgage adviser subtracts from your borrowing capacity. Ask for a modest limit and raise it later if you genuinely need to.

**Paying for insurance you already have.** Premium cards bundle travel insurance, purchase protection and car hire excess cover. If you already hold a Dutch travel policy or your employer provides one, you are paying twice. Read what the card actually covers before paying €175 a year for it.

**Treating rejection as a verdict.** A refusal is a statement about one form on one day, usually about an income threshold or an address. It is not a permanent status, and there is no score that remembers it.

## Which route fits which situation

**You bank with ABN AMRO, ING or Rabobank and want the simplest possible outcome.** Apply through your own bank's app. Lower fee than the premium standalone cards, and the settlement is automatic.

**You want travel insurance and purchase protection bundled in.** Look at the ICS tiers, and compare what they cover against what you already have.

**Your income is variable or freelance.** Expect more questions and be ready with an accountant's figures or several months of statements. Our [ZZP business banking guide](/guides/finance/dutch-business-bank-account-zzp-2026/) covers the wider self-employed picture.

**You arrived last month.** Do not apply yet. Register, open the bank account, get one payslip, and use a multi-currency card in the meantime.

**You mainly wanted a card for foreign spending.** You probably do not want a credit card at all. A multi-currency account solves that problem better and cheaper.
