Two of the cheapest insurance policies in the Netherlands are also the two that expats most often skip. Contents insurance costs a few euros a month. Private liability insurance costs less than a round of coffee. Between them they cover the two most common domestic disasters in Dutch housing: water finding its way into someone else's flat, and your possessions being destroyed or stolen.

The reason people skip them is not stinginess — it is that neither is compulsory, both have Dutch names that resist translation, and the division of responsibility between landlord and tenant is genuinely unclear to newcomers. This guide sorts out who insures what, what the policies actually pay for, roughly what they cost in 2026, and the exclusions that turn a claim into an argument.

## The three policies, and who needs which

### Opstalverzekering — buildings insurance

This covers the structure: walls, roof, floors, fixed installations, the permanently fitted kitchen and bathroom, and usually things like a boiler and built-in wardrobes. Fire, storm, water escape from pipes, and impact damage are the classic insured events.

**Renters do not buy this.** It is the owner's policy. Your landlord — private individual, investor, or housing corporation — holds it, and you never see the document. If a pipe inside the wall bursts and ruins the plasterwork, that is the landlord's insurer's problem.

**Owners must buy it**, in practice from the day of transfer, because Dutch mortgage lenders make it a condition of the loan. In an apartment building, buildings cover is usually arranged collectively by the Vereniging van Eigenaren (VvE, the owners' association) and paid through your monthly VvE contribution — so before you buy an individual policy, check what the VvE already covers. Our [guide to buying a house as an expat](/guides/finance/buying-house-netherlands-expat-guide-2026/) covers where this sits in the purchase process.

### Inboedelverzekering — contents insurance

This covers your movable possessions. Furniture, beds, clothing, laptops and phones, television, kitchen equipment, books, bicycles kept inside, plants, and in most policies a limited amount for valuables such as jewellery and cameras. Insured events typically include fire and smoke, theft and burglary with signs of forced entry, storm, water damage, and vandalism.

Both renters and owners need this. For a renter it is the *only* property policy you hold. It is also the one where expats most often under-insure, for a specific reason: you arrive with two suitcases, decide you have nothing worth insuring, and then furnish an entire apartment over eighteen months without ever revisiting the decision.

Add up what you would have to replace after a fire — a sofa, a bed, a dining set, a laptop, a monitor, a bike, a wardrobe of clothes, a kitchen's worth of equipment — and the number is nearly always five figures.

There is also a specific renter's category worth knowing: **huurdersbelang**, which covers improvements the tenant has installed and paid for, such as a fitted floor or a kitchen upgrade. If you have spent your own money on the apartment, ask about this explicitly, because a standard contents policy may not include it.

### Aansprakelijkheidsverzekering particulieren (AVP) — private liability

This is the policy that covers damage you cause to other people or their property, in daily life rather than at work. It is the most widely held voluntary insurance in the Netherlands and it is cheap because most people never claim.

The scenarios it exists for are mundane and expensive. You leave a tap running and water reaches the flat below. Your child throws a ball through a neighbour's window. You knock over a display in a shop. You cycle into someone and injure them. Dutch liability for personal injury is uncapped in principle, which is why the standard cover levels are so high: commonly €1.25 million, €2.5 million or €5 million per claim. The premium difference between the lower and higher levels is typically small, which is why most advisers suggest not economising on the sum insured.

Two expat-relevant details. First, cover for damage to **rented or borrowed property** is limited or conditional in many policies — precisely the case that matters if you rent. Read that clause. Second, family cover often extends to an unmarried child living away from home solely for full-time study, sometimes up to an age limit and sometimes with narrower cover outside Europe. That is useful for Dutch families; it is much less likely to help you if the family policy sits with an insurer in another country.

## What it costs in 2026

Dutch comparison sites put **contents insurance** broadly between about €3 and €12 per month. The spread comes from postcode (urban risk is priced higher), the type of home, the declared contents value, whether you add extended cover for valuables or glass, and the deductible you accept.

**Private liability insurance** is cheaper: roughly €2 to €5 per month for a single person and around €4 to €8 for a family, depending on the cover level and insurer. Many insurers offer a package discount when contents and liability are bought together, which is generally the sensible route — one renewal date, one insurer to call, slightly lower total premium.

Treat these as market context, not a quote. The only figure that matters is the one you get for your own postcode and household, which is why the comparison step is worth ten minutes.

## Getting the contents value right

The number you declare is the single most consequential entry on the application, because Dutch insurers apply a proportional reduction if you are materially underinsured. Declare €15,000 of contents when the real replacement value is €30,000 and a €4,000 claim can be settled at a fraction of it — even though the loss was well under the sum insured.

Dutch insurers avoid this argument with the **inboedelwaardemeter**, a standardised contents-value calculator based on household composition, age, income and home size. If you complete it honestly through the insurer's own tool, most insurers waive the underinsurance clause. This is one of the few pieces of Dutch insurance bureaucracy that exists purely in the consumer's favour, so use it rather than guessing.

Two adjustments expats should make. Add the value of equipment you brought with you — a good laptop, a camera, an instrument, professional tools — which the standard calculator may not capture. And revisit the figure after any significant purchase or a move to a larger place.

## The exclusions that cause disputes

**Bicycle theft from the street.** Contents policies generally cover a bike stolen from inside the home or a locked private storage room. A bike taken from a public rack is a matter for a separate bicycle insurance policy. In a country where bike theft is a routine event, this is not a hypothetical gap — our guide to [cycling insurance and theft](/blog/cycling-netherlands-expat-insurance-theft-2026/) covers the options.

**Theft without signs of forced entry.** If a burglar enters through a door you left unlocked or a window you left open, insurers may reduce or refuse the claim. Some policies require certified locks.

**Gradual damage and neglect.** Damp that developed over a year, a leak you knew about and did not report, wear and tear — none of it is an insured event. Insurance covers sudden and unforeseen damage.

**Water damage limits.** Escape of water from pipes and appliances is usually covered; water entering from outside is often not. Groundwater and flooding from major watercourses are typically excluded from standard policies, which matters in a country where a great deal of housing sits below sea level. If you live in a basement flat or a known-risk area, ask specifically what your policy says.

**Valuables sub-limits.** Jewellery, watches, art and collections are usually capped at a fixed amount within a contents policy. Above the cap you need to declare individual items and pay a higher premium.

**Items outside the home.** A laptop stolen from a café is often not a contents claim, or is covered only under an extension. Check before you rely on it.

**Business equipment and business liability.** An AVP covers you as a private individual. If you freelance from home, damage or claims arising from your professional work are outside it, and you need business liability cover instead — see our [ZZP registration guide](/blog/zzp-registration-netherlands-step-by-step-2026/) for where that fits.

## How to arrange cover, in the right order

**Step 1 — read your tenancy agreement or your VvE documents.** Some Dutch tenancy contracts oblige the tenant to hold contents or liability cover. Some VvE arrangements already include glass or buildings cover you would otherwise duplicate. Ten minutes here prevents both gaps and double payment.

**Step 2 — complete the contents-value calculator honestly** and note the resulting sum insured.

**Step 3 — compare the market.** Premiums for identical cover vary meaningfully between insurers, and the differences in conditions — water damage, theft requirements, cover for items away from home — matter as much as the price. **Step 5 — photograph everything.** Walk through the apartment with your phone, film each room including inside cupboards, and store the video off-device along with receipts for anything expensive. This is the difference between a smooth claim and a negotiation from memory.

**Step 6 — review after every move.** Your premium is postcode-dependent, and your contents value changes when you move. Notify your insurer of the new address rather than letting the policy quietly cover the wrong home.

## If you need to claim

Report the damage to your insurer as soon as you discover it — policies require prompt notification and late reporting is a common ground for reduction. For theft or vandalism, file a police report (aangifte); insurers ask for the reference number. Keep the damaged items until the insurer confirms you can dispose of them, since an assessor may want to see them.

If liability is involved, do not accept responsibility to the other party in writing before speaking to your insurer. In Dutch practice the insurer assesses liability, and an admission made in a stairwell conversation can complicate that. Pass the other party's details to your insurer and let the two insurers settle it.

If a claim is refused and you disagree, Dutch insurers have an internal complaints procedure, and if that fails you can take the dispute to the financial services complaints institute Kifid at no cost. ## The short version

Liability insurance is the cheapest sensible purchase you will make in the Netherlands: a few euros a month against an uncapped risk. Contents insurance is nearly as cheap and protects a replacement value that grows quietly with every piece of furniture you buy. If you rent, those two policies are your entire property insurance programme; if you own, add buildings cover, most likely through your VvE.
