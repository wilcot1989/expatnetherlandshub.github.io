## Why the exchange rate is the whole story

Every international transfer has two prices. The visible one is the fee. The invisible one is the gap between the rate you are given and the mid-market rate — the rate you see when you search "EUR to INR".

That gap is why two transfers with identical fees can deliver noticeably different amounts in Mumbai, and why comparing providers on fees alone is close to useless. The only honest comparison is: for the exact euro amount I want to send today, how many rupees land in the recipient's account?

Wise's positioning is built on that point. On its Dutch send-money-to-India page it states that it uses the mid-market rate — "de middenkoers, de eerlijkste koers" — and shows costs upfront before you send (wise.com, retrieved 20 August 2026). A bank's FX margin, by contrast, is generally not itemised anywhere on your statement.

## How a Dutch bank transfer compares — without invented numbers

Sending rupees through a Dutch bank means a SWIFT transfer, since India sits outside SEPA. Three cost points apply:

1. **The bank's transfer charge**, published in its tariff sheet (tarievenoverzicht) for non-SEPA payments.
2. **The bank's exchange-rate margin**, applied silently at conversion and not usually shown as a fee.
3. **Correspondent bank deductions**, which can reduce the amount received when the payment passes through intermediary banks.

What your bank charges depends on your bank, your package and the day, so here is what to ask — ideally in writing via your bank's chat, so you have a record:

- What is the charge for a non-SEPA transfer in a foreign currency?
- Which exchange rate is applied, and what is the margin over the mid-market rate?
- Who pays correspondent bank charges — sender, recipient, or shared?
- What is the cut-off time for same-day processing?

Then compare against a live quote for the same amount, using rupees received as the only metric that matters. A five-minute exercise you do once.

## Practical points specific to this corridor

**Get the recipient details exactly right.** IFSC code, account number and name as registered at the Indian bank. A UPI ID suits smaller family transfers; a bank account is the route for larger amounts.

**Choose your purpose code deliberately.** Family maintenance, education, travel and medical are different codes with different documentation expectations on the Indian side. A wrong choice means a delay, sometimes a return.

**Know which account you are sending to.** NRE and NRO accounts behave differently in India for interest taxation and for moving money back out — worth a conversation with your Indian bank if the money is your own.

**Do not confuse this with tax.** Sending money from Dutch taxed income to family in India is not a Dutch tax event in itself, but assets you hold abroad can matter for your Box 3 declaration.

## Which route suits which situation

- **Monthly family support:** a specialist provider with a transparent rate and an upfront fee, paid out to a UPI ID or bank account.
- **One large transfer, such as a property payment:** compare providers *and* your bank on the same day for the same amount, and ask both about limits and documentation.
- **You need paperwork the Indian bank will accept:** ask which confirmation each provider issues before sending.
