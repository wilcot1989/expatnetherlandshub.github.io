Most expats decide about private lease by comparing monthly amounts. That is the least informative number in the arrangement. The monthly amount tells you what you pay when everything goes to plan; the contract tells you what you pay when it does not — when you drive more than expected, scuff a wheel, or leave the country two years into a four-year commitment.

This article is about that second document. > **Quick answer:** Dutch private lease is governed by the general terms of the Keurmerk Private Lease, drawn up with the Consumentenbond, plus each provider's own supplementary terms, which may never be less favourable than the general terms. Four things decide most expat outcomes: the contract is registered with BKR at 100% of the total instalments since 1 April 2022 and eats into your mortgage borrowing room; you get 14 days to withdraw; you can normally only give notice from the start of the second year, against a fee that is a percentage of the remaining instalments; and moving abroad is a ground on which the provider may dissolve the agreement, with a fee attached. Read the supplementary terms before the brochure.

## The Keurmerk Private Lease Is the Actual Rulebook

The Netherlands regulates private lease mostly through self-regulation rather than financial-services law. The Keurmerk Private Lease, introduced by the Dutch vehicle leasing association in 2016, sets standard general terms agreed with the Consumentenbond, the Dutch consumers' association.

Its promise comes as five assurances: fair terms, a complete product, protection against unaffordably high monthly costs, 14 days of cooling-off time, and access to an independent disputes committee. That last one is real rather than decorative — the Geschillencommissie Private Lease issues binding decisions that only a court can override, taking around 22 weeks on average, after you have complained to the provider first.

Two structural points matter more than any marketing:

**There are two layers of terms.** The general terms are identical across all Keurmerk providers. On top of them sit the provider's own *aanvullende voorwaarden* — supplementary terms, which may never deviate to your disadvantage, the general terms prevailing if they do. But they can regulate matters the general terms are silent on, which is exactly where surprises live.

**Check which version applies.** The publicly downloadable general terms are the December 2017 version, and the Keurmerk's participant portal carries a notice about updated terms taking effect on 1 October 2026. Credit registration of private lease is also still voluntary self-regulation: BKR reported in June 2025 that the Ministry of Finance was weighing whether to treat leases without a purchase option as credit under Dutch financial-supervision law.

## What Is Actually Written on the Contract

The Keurmerk specifies what the contract must set out. Hold your quote against it:

- The car, with chosen options and accessories
- The number of kilometres per year
- The included services, such as maintenance and replacement transport
- The monthly lease amount
- The excess-kilometre price, including any maximum
- The own contribution (deductible) in the event of damage
- Any deposit
- Any administration charges for traffic fines
- **The amount that will be registered with BKR as a financial obligation**

That last line is on the contract itself. If you cannot find it, you are not looking at a complete Keurmerk contract.

The monthly amount always includes repair and maintenance, tyre replacement, insurance, replacement transport in the Netherlands (in any case after 72 hours), damage and glass repair, breakdown assistance including abroad, road tax and depreciation, VAT included. It never includes fuel or charging — and everything else that can land on a bill, from excess kilometres to your damage deductible to end-of-contract items, sits outside it too. That is the real total cost of a private lease: a fixed monthly amount plus contingent charges whose ceilings you can read in advance.

## The BKR Registration: Consequences Beyond Cars

When you sign, the provider reports the agreement and its financial obligation to Stichting Bureau Krediet Registratie in Tiel, which records it in the Centraal Krediet Informatiesysteem (CKI) under contract code **OA**. The registered amount changed materially a few years ago:

| Period | Basis of registration |
|---|---|
| Before 1 April 2022 | 65% of the total of the monthly instalments |
| From 1 April 2022 | 100% of the total of the monthly instalments |

The registered obligation follows BKR's own rules and can be lower — never higher — than your actual obligation. Arrears are reported separately, once they last longer than the period set in the BKR regulations. Deregistration is quick but not a clean slate: the provider must deregister within 21 days of the car coming back at the latest, even if the final settlement has not been made, but fail to pay that settlement and an arrears code can still follow afterwards.

### What it means for a Dutch mortgage

The Keurmerk is direct here: in a mortgage application *all* your obligations are scrutinised, private lease costs count, and banks check both your bank statements and BKR. Its own conclusion is balanced rather than alarming — if your finances allow it you can still perfectly well qualify for a mortgage with a lease car — but it advises anyone with moving plans to take advice first. So if a Dutch mortgage is anywhere in the next few years, have an adviser calculate your capacity with and without the lease *before* you sign.

A registration in itself is not a black mark. Many consumers are already registered through a credit card or an overdraft facility, and a phone contract with a "free" handset has counted as a loan since 1 May 2017, as covered in [Dutch phone contracts and BKR registration](/blog/dutch-phone-contracts-bkr-credit-registration-2026/). A registration with no arrears shows you meet your commitments — the logic behind [how to build a credit history in the Netherlands](/blog/how-to-build-credit-history-netherlands-expats-2026/).

Before any of it, the provider runs an affordability check against a *draagkrachtnorm* — the amount that must remain for living costs once fixed costs are paid — and asks for payslips and your other fixed costs. Having no Dutch credit history is not a penalty; you are assessed on income, housing costs and contract type.

## Kilometre Bundles: Where the Quote Gets Optimistic

The lease price is driven largely by annual mileage and contract length, so the temptation when requesting quotes is a low bundle for a low headline price. The bill arrives later.

Excess kilometres are those driven above the agreed number for the period, charged at the excess-kilometre price on the contract. The Keurmerk's worked example: a bundle of 1,000 km per month, an odometer at 12,500 km after twelve months against 12,000 agreed, and a price of EUR 0.20 per kilometre gives 500 x 0.20 = EUR 100 to pay. They are settled each time the lease has run a year and again at the end, and an overrun that shows up mid-year can be settled monthly. If your circumstances change, ask whether the annual mileage can be adjusted — the Keurmerk notes such a change can often be applied retroactively from the start of the contract.

One expat-specific trap: holiday driving. You may take a private lease car abroad, but which countries are permitted is in the supplementary terms and which are insured is on the green card. Two long drives across Europe can put a 10,000 km bundle out of reach without you noticing.

## Damage, Deductibles and the Return Inspection

**Your deductible is capped.** For covered third-party or own-damage claims you pay an own contribution per claim, or the damage amount if that is lower. Supplementary terms may raise the deductible for the rest of the lease if more than two claims occur within 12 months. The general terms cap it at EUR 500 per claim before such an increase and EUR 1,000 after it, and if the damage is recovered from a third party you get your deductible back.

**Wear is defined in a document you can read in advance.** Every provider operates an *innameprotocol* — a return protocol — specifying what counts as normal-use damage, for which no deductible applies, taking mileage and the age of the car into account. It is on the provider's website. Read it before you sign: anything not classified as normal use is on you.

Report damage as soon as you find it, and attend the return inspection: you are not obliged to, but if you later dispute the report, proving it wrong is your burden. Ask for a copy, and hand in everything — spare keys, registration card, green card — because a missing item can be treated as damage.

**No-claim years are not guaranteed.** Not every provider builds up claim-free years for a privately leased car. Ask for a written statement of claims during the lease — you will want it when you insure a car of your own. ## Every Exit Route, In Order of Cost

A lease is easy to enter and structurally hard to leave, so read this before the price list.

**1. The 14-day cooling-off period (free).** You have 14 days to withdraw after the provider receives your signed contract, at no cost, using its withdrawal form or your own clear written statement. You can only collect the car once that period has passed, unless you formally waive it at the provider's premises — waiving it to drive away a week earlier is a poor trade.

**2. Notice from the start of the second year (fee applies).** You can give notice at the earliest against the first day of the second year of the lease period, with at least one month's notice, in writing — or by email if you concluded the contract online. The fee must be paid before the date you gave notice for; if it is not, the notice has no effect and the lease continues.

**3. Notice earlier, if the supplementary terms allow it.** Where they permit earlier notice, those terms set the fee and possibly a longer notice period instead. This is a genuine difference between providers, worth asking about if your stay is uncertain.

**How the fee is calculated.** It can be a fixed percentage of the instalments you would still have had to pay. The Keurmerk's example: 50%, an instalment of EUR 200, a 36-month period, notice against the start of month 25 — leaving 12 x EUR 200 = EUR 2,400, so a fee of EUR 1,200. It can also be based on the actual duration, in which case the supplementary terms must show a worked example. Either way there is a ceiling, and on request the provider gives you a calculation. The fee is not punitive: the monthly amount was priced on the agreed term, and over a shorter run the insurance and road tax costs are not recovered.

**4. Transfer to someone else (not a right).** Transfer to a third party is not regulated within the Keurmerk: the provider decides whether to cooperate and on what conditions, credit-checks your candidate, and may charge administration costs. Treat any "expat transfer option" as a claim to verify in writing.

**5. Moving abroad (dissolution, with a fee).** Moving abroad is one of the grounds on which the lease company may dissolve the agreement, alongside bankruptcy, debt restructuring, theft where the car is not recovered after 30 days, and a total loss. Before dissolving on that ground it must point you to the notice provisions, and you owe a fee equal to the termination fee unless the supplementary terms set a lower one. There is a registration mechanic too: a registered keeper must be registered in the Netherlands, so emigrating forces a change of registration regardless. Leaving the country does not end the contract by operation of law — it gives the provider a right, not you.

**6. What the standard terms do not cover for free.** Losing your driving entitlement through age or illness gives no right to free termination, and nor does unemployment or incapacity for work; some providers add arrangements in their supplementary terms. If you genuinely cannot pay through no fault of your own, the terms oblige the provider to seek a reasonable solution on the fee, such as an instalment arrangement, if you report it with written evidence.

## The Pre-Signature Checklist

1. **Find the BKR amount on the contract**, and check it against any mortgage plan you have.
2. **Read the termination clause in the supplementary terms**: can you give notice before the second year, and on what formula?
3. **Get the fee in numbers** for a realistic exit month — say month 20 of a 48-month contract.
4. **Ask in writing whether transfer is possible**, and at what cost. Silence means no.
5. **Match the term to the shortest realistic version of your stay.** The minimum lease period is 12 months.
6. **Pressure-test the kilometre bundle** with commuting plus holidays, and check the excess price and any cap.
7. **Download the return protocol**, and note the deductible and whether it rises after two claims in 12 months.
8. **Check the deposit** (at most three months' instalments, refunded within 65 days of the end of the lease) **and confirm the provider carries the Keurmerk.** Without the mark, none of the above is contractually yours.

Lease payments run by SEPA direct debit from a Dutch account. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a private lease registered with BKR?**

Yes — under code OA in the CKI, at 100% of the total instalments since 1 April 2022 and 65% before that date. Arrears are reported separately, and the obligation reduces your mortgage borrowing room.

**Can I cancel early, and what does it cost?**

Normally from the first day of the second year, with at least a month's notice, unless the supplementary terms allow earlier. The fee is a percentage of the remaining instalments — the Keurmerk's example uses 50%, giving EUR 1,200 on twelve remaining months of EUR 200 — capped either way.

**What if I leave the Netherlands?**

Moving abroad is a ground for the provider to dissolve the contract, with a fee equal to the termination fee, and the registration has to change because a keeper must be registered in the Netherlands.

## Final Thoughts

Private lease in the Netherlands is a well-documented product, and that is its real advantage: the rules are written down, negotiated with the consumers' association, with a disputes committee behind them. But well-documented is not the same as low-risk for someone whose stay has an end date. Three numbers decide it: the BKR amount on the contract, the fee at a realistic exit month, and the kilometre bundle against how you will actually drive. Get those in writing and the contract holds no surprises. Skip them and the monthly amount you compared so carefully turns out to have been the least important figure in the deal.
