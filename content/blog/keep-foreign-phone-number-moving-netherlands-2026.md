The question arrives in every expat forum in a slightly different form, and it always contains the same hopeful assumption: *can I take my number with me to the Netherlands?* The short answer is no, and it is worth saying plainly before anyone wastes a week on it. Mobile numbers belong to national numbering plans. A Dutch provider can allocate you a Dutch 06 number and nothing else. There is no mechanism, in the EU or outside it, for moving a foreign mobile number onto a Dutch network.

The useful question is the one underneath: how do you avoid losing a number that ten years of banks, friends, family, employers and two-factor systems know about? That has good answers. This guide covers them, and then covers how portability *does* work once you are inside the Dutch system.

> **Quick answer:** You cannot port a foreign number to a Dutch provider. Instead, keep it alive elsewhere: a minimal home-country plan running as a second eSIM line, or the number ported to a VoIP service such as Google Voice for US numbers. Get a Dutch 06 number for daily life, migrate WhatsApp and your two-factor accounts deliberately while both lines still work, and remember that Dutch-to-Dutch portability is free, arranged by your new provider, and usually done within one working day.

## Why numbers do not cross borders

Every country administers its own numbering plan. In the Netherlands that is done under the Telecommunications Act with ACM as the regulator; mobile numbers begin with 06 and are allocated in blocks to licensed Dutch operators. Portability obligations — the right to take your number to a competitor — are national obligations on operators inside that plan. They have no reach outside it, and there is no international clearing arrangement that would let a Dutch operator take custody of, say, a German or Indian mobile number.

This cuts both ways. When you eventually leave the Netherlands, you will not be able to take your 06 number with you either.

What you *can* do is decouple the number from the physical journey. That is the whole trick, and there are three ways to do it.

## Route one: keep the home number on a second line

This is the most common approach and usually the best one for the first year.

Modern phones — iPhone from the XS generation, most recent Pixel and Samsung flagships — support dual SIM, typically one physical SIM plus one eSIM, and increasingly two eSIMs. That lets you carry a Dutch 06 number for daily life and your original number simultaneously on the same device.

**How to set it up before you move:**

1. **Downgrade, do not cancel.** Ask your home provider for their cheapest retention option — a minimal prepaid tariff, a bare SIM-only plan, or an explicit "keep my number" product where one exists. What you need is the number kept active, not the allowance.
2. **Check the dormancy rules.** Prepaid numbers are recycled after a period of inactivity that varies by country and provider, often somewhere between three and twelve months. Find out the exact rule for your provider and diarise a keep-alive action — usually a small top-up or an outgoing call.
3. **Convert to eSIM if you can, while still in the country.** Provider eSIM conversion sometimes requires domestic verification, an in-country SMS or a shop visit. Doing it after you have left can be genuinely difficult.
4. **Set up a payment method that will survive.** Home-country direct debits often break when you close a home-country bank account. Many expats end up paying a foreign carrier bill by card from the Netherlands, where the exchange rate applied by a Dutch bank card quietly costs more than the plan itself. A multi-currency account such as [Wise](https://go.expatnetherlandshub.com/wise?ref=/blog/keep-foreign-phone-number-moving-netherlands-2026/) is a common way to hold a home-currency balance and pay those bills without an FX markup on each small charge.

**How to configure it once here.** Set the Dutch line as default for calls, SMS and mobile data. Set the home line to data off. For a non-EU number this prevents accidental roaming charges; for an EU number it reduces the roaming footprint that could trigger a fair-use review.

**The EU caveat that catches people.** If your home number is from another EU or EEA country, "Roam Like at Home" lets it work in the Netherlands at domestic rates — but the rules are written for travellers. Providers may assess a rolling four-month period: if you spent more time roaming than at home and your roaming usage exceeded your domestic usage, they may ask you to explain within a set period and can then apply surcharges. Living in Amsterdam on a Portuguese plan indefinitely is exactly the pattern those rules describe. Keeping the line minimal and data-free is what makes the arrangement sustainable.

## Route two: move the number to the internet

If the point of the old number is receiving calls and texts rather than being a working phone line, port it out of mobile entirely and into a VoIP or virtual-number service. The number then lives on the internet and reaches you anywhere.

For **US numbers** this route is mature. Google Voice accepts port-ins of eligible US mobile numbers for a one-time fee of $20, after which the number works over data with no monthly cost for personal use. Two important constraints: the port request must be made while the number is still active on a US mobile carrier, and Google requires it to be made from the US, so this is a before-you-fly task, not an after-you-land one. Your existing carrier may also charge its own port-out fee, and if that number was the only line on the account, the account may close when the port completes.

For **other countries**, comparable services exist but vary widely in whether they accept mobile port-ins, and at what price. Search for VoIP providers in your home country that explicitly support mobile number porting, and confirm before you commit.

**The limitation to plan around.** VoIP-hosted numbers do not always receive SMS reliably, and a significant share of banks and government services deliberately refuse to send verification codes to numbers they identify as virtual. If your old number is the anchor for a bank login in your home country, test whether the codes still arrive before you rely on it. This is the single most common way this route disappoints people.

## Route three: let it go, deliberately

Sometimes the honest answer is that the old number is not worth an ongoing cost. If it is not attached to a home-country bank, tax authority, pension provider or property, and your contacts reach you on WhatsApp or Signal anyway, retiring it is a legitimate choice — as long as you do it on purpose rather than by letting a prepaid SIM silently expire.

If that is your plan, do a full migration first. Details in the checklist below.

## Getting a Dutch number

Whatever you do with the old one, you need a Dutch 06 number. It is not optional in practice: DigiD, banks, gemeente correspondence, delivery services, landlords and employers all assume one.

The sequence depends on whether you have a BSN yet. Without one, start prepaid — brands such as Lebara and Lyca activate on a passport and are sold in supermarkets and phone shops. With a BSN, a Dutch address and a Dutch IBAN, you can take a sim-only subscription, which is substantially cheaper per gigabyte. ## Portability *inside* the Netherlands

Once you have a Dutch number, moving it between Dutch providers is straightforward and well protected. This is *nummerbehoud*.

**Your new provider does the work.** You request number retention during sign-up and give your existing number and provider details. The new provider submits the request and cancels your old subscription. This combined process is the switching service; you should not be contacting your old provider yourself to cancel, because doing so out of sequence is one of the few ways to lose the number.

**It is free.** Providers may not charge for taking your number elsewhere. Costs that arise are early-termination costs on the old contract, which are a separate matter.

**It is fast.** The actual switch normally happens within one working day of the agreed date, or on a future date you choose.

**Your old provider cannot block it.** A valid request cannot be refused, and ACM rules do not permit refusal on the basis of a running device credit or remaining contract term. The old provider may send a final invoice for outstanding amounts, including any handset balance, but that is a debt question, not a portability question.

**You have a grace period.** The right to portability runs until one month after the contract's termination date, so a number is not instantly lost the day a contract ends. Do not rely on this as a plan; do rely on it if something has gone wrong.

**If it fails.** Ask both providers for a written explanation, submit a complaint to your new provider, and escalate to ACM ConsuWijzer, the consumer arm of the regulator, if it is not resolved.

## The migration checklist

Whichever route you take, the number change itself is the risky moment. Work through this while both lines are still active.

**Before you move**

- Convert the home number to eSIM if your provider allows it, or arrange the cheapest retention tariff.
- If you are porting to VoIP, submit the port request while still in the home country and on an active line.
- Export your contacts to an account, not to a SIM card.
- List every account that uses the old number for login or verification: banks, brokerages, tax portal, pension, insurers, employer HR, cloud accounts, marketplaces, airlines.

**In the first weeks here**

- Activate a Dutch prepaid number immediately on arrival so that you can complete the gemeente registration and bank onboarding.
- Use WhatsApp's change-number function while the old line is still receiving SMS. It migrates chats and notifies contacts.
- Move critical two-factor authentication off SMS and onto an authenticator app. This removes the dependency on any single number and is the durable fix.
- Update your Dutch-facing accounts as they are created — DigiD, bank, health insurer, employer — with the Dutch number from the start.

**Once your BSN and Dutch IBAN are in place**

- Switch from prepaid to a sim-only plan, porting the Dutch prepaid number if you have already given it out.
- Review whether the home-country line still earns its cost. If nothing important depends on it, close it deliberately.
- Set a keep-alive reminder if you are retaining it.

## What we would do

For a stay of a year or more, the workable setup is: Dutch 06 number as the primary line on a sim-only plan, home number retained as a second eSIM on the cheapest possible tariff with data disabled, two-factor authentication moved to an app so neither number is load-bearing, and a calendar reminder to review the home line annually. If the home number is American and you mainly need to receive calls, porting it to Google Voice before you leave is cheaper than years of retention fees and removes the dormancy risk altogether.

What does not work, and is worth repeating one final time, is walking into a Dutch phone shop and asking them to transfer a foreign number. No provider in the country can do it.
